\subsection{Linear complex structures}

\bdefn

    A {\emphcolor (linear) complex structure} on a real vector space is an automorphism
    $$ J\colon V\to V \qquad\hbox{such that}\qquad J^2 = -I . $$
    Denote the space of linear complex structures over $V$ by
    $$ \J(V) \subseteq \endo(V) . $$

\edefn

The archetypal linear complex structure is
$$ J_0 = \pmatrix{0&-I\cr I&0} $$
over $\bR^{2n}$.

$V$ having a complex structure is equivalent to it having the structure of a $\bC$-vector space compatible with its real vector space structure.
This is equivalent to the existence of a ring map $\bC\to\endo(V)$ which is a lifting of the ring map $\bR\to\endo(V)$ which defines $V$'s real linear structure:

\diagram{
    \bR&\endo(V)\cr
    \bC
}{
    \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={1,2}}
    \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={2,1}, left cap={(}}
    \diagarrow{from={2,1}, to={1,2}}
}

That is, the existence of a map of $\bR$-algebras $\bC\to\endo(V)$.
Because $\bC$ is generated by $i$ as a real algebra, this is equivalent to the existence of $J\in\endo(V)$ such that $J^2=-I$.
Spelling this out, if $V$ has a complex structure then it becomes a complex vector space with multiplication
$$ \bC\times V\to V,\qquad (a+ib,v) \mapsto av + bJv . $$

Notice that this necessitates that $V$ has even real dimension.
This can be seen because
$$ \dim_\bR V = \dim_\bC V\cdot\dim_\bR\bC = 2\dim_\bC V $$
and because $J^2=-I$ means that $\det J^2=(-1)^{\dim V}$ is positive so $\dim V$ is even.

\bprop

    Let $V$ be a $2n$-dimensional real vector space with complex structure $J$.
    Then there is a vector space isomorphism $\Phi\colon\bR^{2n}\to V$ such that $J\Phi=\Phi J_0$.

\eprop

\bproof

    Viewing $V$ as a complex vector space, there is a basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$.
    Then $v_1,\dots,v_n,Jv_1,\dots,Jv_n$ is a basis of the real vector space $V$.
    Mapping the standard basis $e_1,\dots,e_n,f_1,\dots,f_n$ to this basis gives the desired result.
    \qed

\eproof

Note that the map $\Phi\colon\endo(V)\to\endo(V)$ given by $A\mapsto A^2$ is smooth, and its differential at $A$ is $X\mapsto AX+XA$.
For $A\in\J(V)$ we have that
$$ \ker\d\Phi_A = \set{X\in\endo(V)}[AX+XA=0] , $$
which we claim has dimension $2n^2$.
We can take $V=\bR^{2n}$ and since there is an automorphism mapping $A$ to $J_0$, we may assume $A=J_0$.
Then
$$ \pmatrix{X&Y\cr Z&W}\pmatrix{0&-I\cr I&0} + \pmatrix{0&-I\cr I&0}\pmatrix{X&Y\cr Z&W} = \pmatrix{Y&-X\cr W&-Z} + \pmatrix{-Z&-W\cr X&Y}
= \pmatrix{Y-Z&-X-W\cr X+W&Y-Z} . $$
And this is zero iff $Z=Y$ and $W=-X$, so we have $2n^2$ degrees of freedom as desired.

Thus $\Phi$ has constant rank $(2n)^2-2n^2=2n^2$ on $\J(V)=\Phi^{-1}(-I)$, which means that $\J(V)$ is an embedded submanifold of $\endo(V)$ of
dimension $2n^2$.

A linear complex structure $J$ on $V$ induces an orientation as follows.
Let $v_1,\dots,v_n\in V$ form a complex basis so that $v_1,Jv_1,\dots,v_n,Jv_n$ forms a real basis.
This real basis is the orientation.
This orientation is well-defined, as in it is not dependent on $v_i$.
Let $u_1,\dots,u_n$ be another complex basis, and let $\Phi$ be the complex map $v_i\mapsto u_i$.
Let us write
$$ u_i = \sum_j a_{ij}v_j + b_{ij}Jv_j,\qquad Ju_i = \sum_j a_{ij}Jv_j - b_{ij}v_j $$
so that the representation of the base transfer matrix (of $v_1,\dots,v_n,Jv_1,\dots,Jv_n\to u_1,\dots,u_n,Ju_1,\dots,Ju_n$) is
$$ \pmatrix{A&-B\cr B&A} \oto A+iB , $$
and we know that this has determinant $\abs{\det(A+iB)}^2=\abs{\det\Phi}^2>0$, so they are compatibly oriented.
Note here that we permuted the oriented basis, but we did this for both using the same permutation, so the transfer matrix has the same
determinant.

A linear complex structure is {\emphcolor compatible} with an orientation if it induces that orientation.
For example $J_0$ induces the standard orientation on $\bR^{2n}$.

\bprop

    \benum
        \item $\J(\bR^{2n})$ is diffeomorphic to $\gl_{2n}(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$ and thus has two connected components.
        \item The connected component $\J^+(\bR^{2n})$ containing $J_0$ is the space of all complex structures which are compatible with the standard
        orientation.
        It is thus diffeomorphic to $\gl^+_{2n}(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$ and is homotopy equivalent to
        $$ \J^+(\bR^{2n})\cap\sorth(2n) \cong \frac{\sorth(2n)}{\unitary(n)} $$
        of orthogonal complex structures which are compatible with the standard orientation.
        \item $\J^+(\bR^4)$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^2$.
    \eenum

\eprop

\bproof

    Consider the smooth map
    $$ \gl_{2n}(\bR) \to \J(\bR^{2n}),\qquad \Psi\mapsto\Psi J_0\Psi^{-1} . $$
    By the above lemma this is a surjection, and it quotients to a bijective smooth $\gl_{2n}(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)\to\J(\bR^{2n})$.
    Both of these manifolds have dimension $2n^2$ and the above map is a submersion, so its quotient is a submersion as well,
    thus the quotient is a bijective submersion between manifolds of the same dimension, and thus a diffeomorphism.

    The connected components of $\gl_{2n}(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$ correspond to the connected components of $\gl_{2n}(\bR)$, which are determined
    by the sign of the determinant, equivalently the oriented and non-oriented matrices.
    As $\det J=1$, it lies in $\gl_{2n}^+(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$.

    Now $\orth(2n)/\unitary(n)\inj\gl_{2n}(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$ is a strong deformation retract, via the homotopy
    $$ H\colon[0,1]\times\gl_{2n}(\bR) \to \gl_{2n}(\bR),\qquad H(t,\Psi) = \Psi(\Psi^\top\Psi)^{-t/2} . $$
    This homotopy preserves the quotients, so it descends into them giving the desired homotopy.
    The connected component $\gl_{2n}^+(\bR)/\gl_n(\bC)$ corresponds to $\sorth(2n)/\unitary(n)\cong\J^+(\bR^{2n})\cap\sorth(2n)$.

    For (3) let $n=2$ and let $e_0,e_1,e_2,e_3$ be the standard basis of $\bR^4$ and denote a vector by $x=(x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3)$.
    Then a matrix
    $$ J \in \J(\bR^4)\cap\sorth(4) $$
    is determined by $Je_0$.
    And $J$ satisfies $\iprod{v,Jv}=0$ because $J$ is orthogonal and similar to $J_0$, they are orthogonally similar, and because this identity
    holds for $J_0$ it holds for $J$.
    Thus $e_0,Je_0$ form an orthonormal basis of a $2$-plane $E\subseteq\bR^4$.

    Now for any unit $v\in E^\perp$, $v,Jv$ form an orthonormal basis of $E^\perp$.
    And because $J$ is compatible with the standard orientation, $e_0,Je_0,v,Jv$ is positively oriented.
    There are only two unit vectors in $E^\perp$ orthogonal to $v$, and only one makes the basis oriented.
    So $Jv$ is uniquely defined.

    Thus $\J^+(\bR^4)\cap\sorth(2n)\to S^2$ given by $J\mapsto Je_0$ is a continuous bijection, and is seen to be a homeomorphism.
    Thus $\J^+(\bR^4)$ is homotopy equivalent to $S^2$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bdefn

    Let $(V,\omega)$ be a symplectic space.
    Then a complex structure $J\in\J(V)$ is {\emphcolor compatible} with $\omega$ if
    $$ J^*\omega = \omega,\qquad \omega(v,Jv) > 0 \hbox{ for all $v\in V$} . $$
    Denote the space of compatible complex structures on $(V,\omega)$ by $\J(V,\omega)$.

\edefn

If $J$ is compatible with $\omega$ then
$$ g_J(v,u) = \omega(v,Ju) $$
is an inner product on $V$, and $J$ is skew-adjoint with respect to this inner product: $g_J(v,Ju)=-g_J(Jv,u)$.

\bprop[name={compatcompstruct}]

    Let $(V,\omega)$ be a symplectic space and $J$ a complex structure on $V$.
    The following are then equivalent.
    \benum
        \item $J$ is compatible with $\omega$.
        \item $(V,\omega)$ has a symplectic basis of the form
        $$ v_1,\dots,v_n,Jv_1,\dots,Jv_n . $$
        \item There exists a vector space isomorphism $\Psi\colon\bR^{2n}\to V$ such that
        $$ \Psi^*\omega = \omega_0,\qquad \Psi^*J = J_0 . $$
        \item $J$ satisfies $\omega(v,Jv)>0$ and
        $$ \Lambda \in \L(V,\omega) \implies J\Lambda \in \L(V,\omega) . $$
    \eenum

\eprop

\bproof

    Suppose (1), and let $\Lambda\subseteq V$ be a Lagrangian subspace.
    Choose an orthonormal basis $v_1,\dots,v_n$ of $\Lambda$ with respect to $g_J$, then
    $$ \omega(v_i,Jv_j) = g_J(v_i,v_j) = \delta_{ij},\qquad \omega(Jv_i,Jv_j) = \omega(v_i,v_j) = 0 $$
    (remember that $v_i\in\Lambda$ and $\omega$ is trivial on $\Lambda$).
    Thus $v_1,\dots,v_n,Jv_1,\dots,Jv_n$ forms a symplectic basis as desired.

    (2) then implies (3) by mapping
    $$ \Psi(z) = \sum_{i=1}^n(x_iv_i + y_iJv_i),\qquad z = (x_1,\dots,x_n,y_1,\dots,y_n) . $$
    (3) is easily seen to imply (1).

    Now (1) implies (4), because $J\Lambda$ has the same dimension of $\Lambda$ and for $v,u\in\Lambda$,
    $$ \omega(Jv,Ju) = \omega(u,v) = 0 $$
    so that $J\Lambda$ is isotropic and thus Lagrangian.

    Now (4) implies (1): $g_J(v,u)=\omega(v,Ju)$ is positive definite by assumption, so we just need to show that it is symmetric.
    Suppose that $g_J$ is not symmetric, then there are $v,u\in V$ such that
    $$ \omega(v,Ju) \neq \omega(u,Jv) . $$
    Then $v$ is nonzero so $\omega(v,Jv)>0$, and define
    $$ w = u - frac{\omega(v,Ju)}{\omega(v,Jv)}v . $$
    Then
    $$ \omega(v,Jw)  = 0,\qquad \omega(w,Jv) = \omega(u,Jv) - \omega(v,Ju) \neq 0 . $$
    Thus $w,Jv$ are linearly independent and thus so too are $Jw,v$.
    Because $\omega(Jw,v)=0$, we can extend $v,Jw$ to a Lagrangian subspace $\Lambda$.
    Then $J\Lambda$ is Lagrangian, but $Jv,w\in J\Lambda$ have nonzero $\omega$.
    \qed

\eproof

We now provide three proofs of the fact that $\J(V,\omega)$ is contractible.
We do this in three different ways:
\benum
    \item The first method identifies $\J(V,\omega)$ with the space of symmetric, positive definite, and symplectic matrices.
    Then this is shown to be contractible.

    \item The second method constructs an explicit homotopy equivalence between $\J(V,\omega)$ and the space $\Met(V)$ of all inner products
    on $V$.
    This is based on \refmath[proposition]{homotopymapscompsymp} which shows that $\J(V)$ is homotopy equivalent to the space of nondegenerate
    skew-symmetric bilinear forms on $V$.
    This method will show that every compact subgroup of $\Sp(2n)$ is conjugate to a subgroup of $\unitary(n)$.

    \item The third method identifies $\J(V,\omega)$ with the Siegel upper half space.
\eenum

\bprop

    Let $(V,\omega)$ be a $2n$-dimensional symplectic space.
    Then $\J(V,\omega)$ is diffeomorphic to $\Sym_+(2n)\cap\Sp(2n)$, which is contractible.

\eprop

\bproof

    We may assume that $V=\bR^{2n}$ and $\omega=\omega_0$ in lieu of the previous theorem.
    Then $J\in\J(\bR^{2n},\omega_0)$ iff
    $$ J^2 = -1,\qquad J^\top J_0J = J_0,\qquad \iprod{v,-J_0Jv} = v^\top(-J_0J)v > 0 . $$
    Thus
    $$ (J_0J)^\top = -J^\top J_0 = -J_0J^{-1} = J_0J , $$
    and so
    $$ P = -J_0J $$
    is symmetric, positive definite, and symplectic (because $J_0,J$ are symplectic).
    Conversely if $P$ has these properties then $J=J_0P$ is a compatible complex structure.
    These maps are both smooth inverses, so we have the desired diffeomorphism.

    And $\Sym_+(2n)\cap\Sp(2n)$ is contractible by the null-homotopy
    $$ H(t,P) = P^t , $$
    which is continuous by \refmath[lemma]{expiscont} and well-defined by \refmath[lemma]{expofsymp}.
    This is a null homotopy to the point $I$ as $H_0(\blank)=I$.
    \qed

\eproof

For the second proof we show that $\J(V)$ is homotopy equivalent to the space of symplectic forms on $V$:
$$ \S(V) = \set{\omega\in\Lambda^2(V^*)}[\hbox{$\omega$ is nondegenerate}] . $$
The homotopy equivalence is given explicitly, depending only on a choice of inner product on $V$.
Define
$$ \Met(V) = \set{g\in\Sym^2(V^*)}[\hbox{$g$ is positive definite}] , $$
the space of all inner products on $V$.
$\Met(V)$ is an open convex subspace of $\Sym^2(V^*)$: it is convex because the positive multiples and sums of inner products are inner products,
it is open because $\Sym_+(n)\subseteq\Sym(n)$ is (a similar agument to the proof of \refmath[lemma]{expiscont} shows that for a positive definite
matrix $A$ there is $0<m$ and a neighborhood $U$ in $\Sym$ such that all matrices in $U$ have eigenvalues bound below by $m$, thus are
positive definite).
Because $\Met(V)$ is convex, it is contractible.

For $g\in\Met(V)$ define
$$ \J(V,g) = \set{J\in\J(V)}[J^*g=g],\qquad \S(V,g) = \set{g(J\blank,\blank)}[J\in\J(V,g)] . $$
Notice that $J^*g=g$ iff $\omega_g=g(J\blank,\blank)\in\S(V)$: if $J^*g=g$ then
$$ \omega_g(v,u) = g(Jv,u) = g(-v,Ju) = -g(Ju,v) = -\omega_g(u,v) , $$
and if $\omega_g\in\S(V)$ then
$$ g(Ju,Jv) = \omega_g(u,Jv) = -\omega_g(Jv,u) = -g(-v,u) = g(v,u) = g(u,v) . $$
And so $\S(V,g)\subseteq\S(V)$.

Elements of $\J(V,g)$ and $\S(V,g)$ are said to be {\emphcolor compatible with $g$}.

Now consider the map
$$ \Met(V)\times\J(V) \to \S(V),\qquad (g,J)\mapsto\omega_{g,J} = \frac12(g(J\blank,\blank)-g(\blank,J\blank)) . $$
This is well-defined as $\omega_{g,J}$ is clearly antisymmetric bilinear, and it is nondegenerate
$$ \omega_{g,J}(v,Jv) = \frac12(g(Jv,Jv) + g(v,v)) > 0 . $$
Furthermore this map is smooth as it can be seen as evaluating a polynomial at a matrix.
Recall that $\gl(V)$ acts on $T^2(V^*)$ (bilinear forms on $V$) by $(\Psi^*\alpha)(v,u)=\alpha(\Psi^{-1}v,\Psi^{-1}u)$, and this is a smooth action which restricts to $\Met(V)$ and $\S(V)$.
The map above is then $\gl(V)$-equivariant:
$$ \multline{
    \omega_{\Psi^*g,\Psi^*J}(v,u) = \frac12((\Psi^*g)(\Psi^*Jv,u) - (\Psi^*g)(v,\Psi^*Ju)) = \frac12(g(J\Psi^{-1}v,\Psi^{-1}u) - g(\Psi^{-1}v,J\Psi^{-1}u))\cr
    = \omega_{g,J}(\Psi^{-1}v,\Psi^{-1}u) = (\Psi^*\omega_{g,J})(v,u) .
} $$

For a fixed $g\in\Met(V)$ this map restricts to $f_g\colon\J(V)\to\S(V)$ by $f_g(J)=\omega_{g,J}$.
And for $J\in\J(V,g)$ we have that $g(J\bul,\bul)\in\S(V,g)$ meaning $g(\bul,J\bul)=-g(J\bul,\bul)$ so that $f_g(J)=g(J\bul,\bul)\in\S(V,g)$.
Thus $f_g\colon\J(V,g)\to\S(V,g)$ is a bijective $\gl(V)$-equivariant smooth map, and thus a diffeomorphism.
And so we have the following commutative diagram

\diagram{
    \J(V,g)&\S(V,g)\cr
    \J(V)&\S(V)
}{
    \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={1,2}, text={f_g}, y distance=.25cm}
    \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={1,2}, text={\sim}, y distance=-.15cm}
    \diagarrow{from={2,1}, to={2,2}, text={f_g}, y distance=.25cm}
    \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={2,1}, left cap={(}}
    \diagarrow{from={1,2}, to={2,2}, left cap={(}}
}

\bprop[name={homotopymapscompsymp}]

    Let $V$ be an even-dimensional real vector space.
    Then there exists a $\gl(V)$-equivariant smooth map
    $$ \Met(V) \times \S(V) \to \J(V),\qquad (g,\omega) \mapsto J_{g,\omega} $$
    with the following properties.
    \benum
        \item For $g\in\Met(V),\omega\in\S(V),J\in\J(V)$, we have
        $$ J_{g,\omega} \in \J(V,\omega)\cap\J(V,g),\qquad J\in\J(V,\omega_{g,J}) $$
        and if $g = \omega(\bul,J\bul)$ then
        $$ J_{g,\omega} = J,\qquad \omega_{g,J} = \omega . $$
        Thus for $g\in\Met(V)$, $\J(V,g)\to\S(V,g)$ mapping $J\mapsto\omega_{g,J}$ has inverse $\S(V,g)\to\J(V,g)$ mapping $\omega\mapsto J_{g,\omega}$.

        \item For every $g\in\Met(V)$, the map
        $$ \S(V) \to \J(V),\qquad \omega\mapsto J_{g,\omega} $$
        is a homotopy equivalence with inverse
        $$ \J(V) \to \S(V),\qquad J \mapsto \omega_{g,J} . $$

        \item For every $g\in\Met(V)$, the inclusions $\J(V,g)\inj\J(V)$ and $\S(V,g)\inj\S(V)$ are homotopy equivalences with inverses
        $$ \displaylines{
            \J(V) \to \J(V,g),\qquad J\mapsto J_{g,\omega_{g,J}}\cr
            \S(V) \to \S(V,g),\qquad \omega\mapsto \omega_{g,J_{g,\omega}}.
        } $$
    \eenum

\eprop

\bproof

    First we construct this map.
    For $g\in\Met(V)$ and $\omega\in\S(V)$ define $A_{g,\omega}\in\gl(V)$ by
    $$ g(A_{g,\omega}v,u) = \omega(v,u) . $$
    So $A_{g,\omega}$ is $g$-skew-adjoint and thus $-A^2_{g,\omega}$ is $g$-self-adjoint and $g$-positive definite.
    This means there exists a unique $g$-self-adjoint and $g$-positive definite automorphism $Q_{g,\omega}\colon V\to V$ such that $Q_{g,\omega}^2=-A^2_{g,\omega}$ (consider the Euclidean case).
    By \refmath[proposition]{expiscont}, this is a smooth map.
    Note that $Q_{g,\omega}$ and $A_{g,\omega}$ commute (because $A_{g,\omega}^{-1}Q_{g,\omega}A_{g,\omega}$ is also $g$-self-adjoint and $g$-positive definite, so it is equal to $Q_{g,\omega}$).

    So define
    $$ J_{g,\omega} = Q^{-1}_{g,\omega}A_{g,\omega} \in \J(V,\omega)\cap\J(V,g) . $$
    This is indeed a complex structure as
    $$ J^2_{g,\omega} = A_{g,\omega}^2Q^{-2}_{g,\omega} = -I , $$
    and it is $\omega$- and $g$-compatible as
    $$ g(J_{g,\omega}\bul,J_{g,\omega}\bul) = g(A_{g,\omega}\bul,Q^{-2}_{g,\omega}A_{g,\omega}\bul) = g(\bul,\bul) $$
    and
    $$ \omega(J_{g,\omega}\bul,J_{g,\omega}\bul) = g(A_{g,\omega}J_{g,\omega}\bul,J_{g,\omega}\bul) = g(A_{\omega}\bul,\bul) = \omega(\bul,\bul) , $$
    finally
    $$ \omega(\bul,J_{g,\omega}\bul) = g(A_{g,\omega}\bul,J_{g,\omega}\bul) = -g(Q^{-1}_{g,\omega}A_{g,\omega}^2\bul,\bul) > 0 $$
    as both $-A_{g,\omega}^2$ and $Q_{g,\omega}^{-1}$ are $g$-positive definite.

    The map $(g,\omega)\mapsto J_{g,\omega}$ is smooth, we show that it is also $\gl(V)$-equivariant.
    For $\Psi\in\gl(V)$, first we see that $A_{\Psi^*g,\Psi^*\omega}=\Psi^*A_{g,\omega}=\Psi A_{g,\omega}\Psi^{-1}$:
    $$ (\Psi^*g)(\Psi A_{g,\omega}\Psi^{-1}v,u) = g(A_{g,\omega}\Psi^{-1}v,\Psi^{-1}u) = \omega(\Psi^{-1}v,\Psi^{-1}u) = (\Psi^*\omega)(v,u) . $$
    And $\Psi^*Q_{g,\omega}$ is $\Psi^*g$-self adjoint and positive definite and squaring gives $-\Psi^*A_{g,\omega}^2=-(\Psi^*A_{g,\omega})^2$.
    Thus
    $$ J_{\Psi^*g,\Psi^*\omega} = (\Psi^*Q_{g,\omega})^{-1}(\Psi^*A_{g,\omega}) = \Psi^*(Q_{g,\omega}^{-1}A_{g,\omega}) = \Psi^*J_{g,\omega} $$
    as desired.

    Now we show that this construction satisfies (1): we already showed that $J_{g,\omega}\in\J(V,\omega)\cap\J(V,g)$.
    And for $g=\omega(\bul,J\bul)$, we have that $A_{g,\omega}=J$ as
    $$ g(Jv,u) = g(u,Jv) = \omega(u,J^2v) = -\omega(u,v) = \omega(v,u) , $$
    then $Q=I$ is $g$-symmetric and positive definite and satisfies $Q^2=-A^2$, so $J_{g,\omega}=J$ as desired.

    Now we need to show (2).
    For a set $g\in\Met(V)$, the composite
    $$ \psi\colon \S(V) \xtos{\omega\mapsto J_{g,\omega}} \J(V) \xtos{J\mapsto\omega_{g,J}} \S(V),\qquad \omega\mapsto\omega_{g,J_{g,\omega}} $$
    is homotopic to the identity via the homotopy
    $$ \psi_\bul\colon [0,1]\times\S(V) \to\S(V),\qquad \psi_t(\omega) = (1-t)\omega + t\omega_{g,J_{g,\omega}} . $$
    This is well-defined as $J_{g,\omega}\in\J(V,\omega)\cap\J(V,\omega_{g,J_{g,\omega}})$, so it is compatible with both of these symplectic structures, thus $\psi_t(\omega)(v,J_{g,\omega}v)>0$ and so
    $\psi_t(\omega)$ is symplectic for all $t$.
    As $\psi_0=\id$ and $\psi_1=\psi$ we have the desired homotopy.

    And consider the composite
    $$ \phi\colon \J(V) \xtos{J\mapsto\omega_{g,J}} \S(V) \xtos{\omega\mapsto J_{g,\omega}} \J(V),\qquad J \mapsto J_{g,\omega_{g,J}} . $$
    This homotopic to the identity via $\phi_\bul\colon[0,1]\times\J(V)\to\J(V)$ by
    $$ \phi_t(J) = J_{g_t,\omega_{g_t,J}},\qquad g_t = \frac{1+t}2g + \frac{1-t}2J^*g . $$
    Because $g_1=g$ we see that $\phi_1(J)=J_{g,\omega_{g,J}}=\phi(J)$.
    And $g_0=\frac12(g+J^*g)$ is compatible with $J$ (because $(-I)^*g=g$), and thus also with
    $$ \omega_0 = \omega_{g_0,J} = \frac12(g_0(J\bul,\bul) - g_0(\bul,J\bul)) = \frac12(g(J\bul,\bul) - g(J\bul,\bul)) = \omega_{g,J} $$
    so that
    $$ \psi_0(J) = J_{g_0,\omega_0} = J $$
    as desired.

    For (3), for $\omega\in\S(V,g)$ there is a $J\in\J(V)$ such that $g=\omega(\bul,J\bul)$, thus
    $$ J_{g,\omega} = J,\qquad \omega_{g,J_{g,\omega}} = \frac12(g(J\bul,\bul) - g(\bul,J\bul)) = \omega . $$
    And so the composition $\S(V,g)\inj\S(V)\to\S(V,g)$ is the identity.
    The other direction of the composition is $\psi$, which is homotopic to the identity as we already showed.

    Similarly for $J\in\J(V,g)$, we have that
    $$ \omega_{g,J} = g(J\bul,\bul) \implies g = \omega_{g,J}(\bul,J\bul) $$
    and so
    $$ J_{g,\omega_{g,J}} = J $$
    so that $\J(V,g)\inj\J(V)\to\J(V,g)$ is the identity, and the other direction is $\phi$, which is homotopic to the identity.
    \qed

\eproof

\bcoro[name={compatlincompcontract}]

    For every $\omega\in\S(V)$, there is a homotopy equivalence
    $$ \J(V,\omega) \xtof{J\mapsto g_J}[g\mapsto J_{g,\omega}] \Met(V),\qquad g_J = \omega(\bul,J\bul) . $$
    Thus $\J(V,\omega)$ is contractible.

\ecoro

\bproof

    By the above proposition, $J_{g_J,\omega}=J$ so one composition is the identity.
    The other composition is $\Met(V)\to\Met(V)$ given by $g\mapsto g_{J_{g,\omega}}$, which is homotopic to the identity because $\Met(V)$ is convex.
    \qed

\eproof

\bnote

    If $X\subseteq V$ is a convex subset of a topological vector space $V$, then every map $f\colon X\to X$ is homotopic to the identity via linear interpolation:
    $$ \phi_t(x) = tf(x) + (1-t)x . $$

\enote

We now need to briefly explain Haar measures.
Let $V,W$ be normed real vector spaces of potentially infinite dimension.
A linear map $F\colon V\to W$ is {\emphcolor bounded} if there is some real $C\geq0$ such that
$$ \norm{F(v)}_W \leq C\norm v_V\qquad\hbox{for all $v\in V$} . $$
This is equivalent to the following quantity being finite:
$$ \norm F_\op = \sup_{0\neq v\in V}\frac{\norm{Fv}_W}{\norm v_V} = \sup_{\norm v_V=1}\norm{Fv}_W < \infty . $$
This makes the space of bounded linear maps $\hom(V,W)$ into a normed vector space, as is easily verified.

The {\emphcolor Haar measure} on a compact topological group $G$ is the unique bounded linear functional
$$ C(G,\bR)\to\bR,\qquad f \mapsto \int_Gf\d\mu $$
from the space of continuous maps $C(G,\bR)$ (with the supremum norm), which is invariant under both left and right translations, and the Haar measure of the constant function $1$ is $1$.
By invariance under left and right translations, we mean
$$ \int_Gf(\Phi\bul)\d\mu = \int_Gf(\bul\Phi)\d\mu = \int_Gf(\bul)\d\mu\qquad\hbox{ for all $\Phi\in G$} . $$

The reason for calling this the Haar {\it measure\/} is because it can be, and commonly is, constructed as a measure $\mu$ over the Borel subsets of $G$ which is unique in that it has certain properties,
and the linear functional is just the Lebesgue integral of $f$ relative to $\mu$.
Thus we can utilize results regarding the Lebesgue integral when discussing the Haar measure.

We will now prove the following proposition with the help of some lemmas.

\bprop[name={compspconj}]

    Every compact subgroup $G\subseteq\Sp(2n)$ is conjugate to a subgroup of $\unitary(n)$.

\eprop

\blemm

    There exists a symmetric positive definite $S\in\Mat_{2n}$ such that $\Psi^\top S\Psi=S$ for all $\Psi\in G$.

\elemm

\bproof

    For all pairs of indices $i,j$, define the continuous linear functional $\pi_{i,j}\colon\Mat_{2n}\to\bR$ mapping a matrix to its value at that coordinate, $A\mapsto A_{ij}$.
    Then define
    $$ S_{ij} = \int_{\Psi\in G}\pi_{ij}(\Psi^\top\Psi)\d\mu . $$
    Because $\Psi^\top\Psi$ is symmetric, we see immediately that $S$ is.
    And $S$ is positive definite: for $v=(v_1,\dots,v_{2n})$ we have
    $$ v^\top Sv = \sum_{i,j}v_i S_{ij}v_j = \sum_{i,j}\int_{\Psi\in G}v_i\pi_{ij}(\Psi^\top\Psi)v_j = \int_{\Psi\in G}v^\top\Psi^\top\Psi v . $$
    Because $\Psi^\top\Psi$ is positive-definite, this is the integral of a positive function and is thus positive, so $S$ is positive-definite.

    A similar computation shows that for every $\Phi\in G$ we have
    $$ \Phi^\top S\Phi = \int_{\Psi\in G}\Phi^\top\Psi^\top\Psi\Phi\d\mu = \int_{\Psi\in G}(\Psi\Phi)^\top(\Psi\Phi)\d\mu = S $$
    because of the left invariance of the Haar measure.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    There is an $\omega_0$-compatible complex structure $J\in\J(\bR^{2n},\omega_0)$ which commutes with every element of $G$.

\elemm

\bproof

    Let $S$ be as in the above lemma, and let $g=\iprod{\bul,S\bul}\in\Met(\bR^{2n})$ be the inner product determined by $S$.
    Then both $g$ and $\omega_0$ are $G$-invariant, and thus so is $J_{g,\omega}$ from \refmath[proposition]{homotopymapscompsymp}: for $\Psi\in G$,
    $$ \Psi^*J_{g,\omega} = J_{\Phi^*g,\Phi^*\omega} = J_{g,\omega} . \qed $$

\eproof

\bproof[\refmath{compspconj}]

    Let $J$ be as in the previous lemma, so there is a vector space isomorphism $\Phi\colon\bR^{2n}\to\bR^{2n}$ such that $\Phi^*\omega_0=\omega_0$ and $\Phi^*J=J_0$.
    Thus $\Phi\in\Sp(2n)$ and $\Phi J\Phi^{-1}=J_0$.
    For every $\Psi\in G$, $\Phi\Psi\Phi^{-1}\in\Sp(2n)$ commutes with $J_0$ and thus is in $\unitary(n)$.
    So we have
    $$ \Phi G\Phi^{-1} \subseteq \unitary(n) $$
    as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bremark[name={compatpairs}]

    Let $V$ be even-dimensional and define
    $$ \C(V) = \set{(\omega,J)}[\omega\in\S(V),\ J\in\J(V,\omega)] , $$
    the space of compatible pairs.
    The projections $\pi_\S\colon\C(V)\to\S(V)$ and $\pi_\J\colon\C(V)\to\J(V)$ are then homotopy equivalences.
    Indeed for $g\in\Met(V)$, the maps
    $$ \displaylines{
        \iota_\S\colon\S(V)\to\C(V),\qquad \iota_\S(\omega) = (\omega,J_{g,\omega})\cr
        \iota_\J\colon\J(V)\to\C(V),\qquad \iota_\J(J) = (\omega_{g,J},J)
    } $$
    are homotopy inverses of $\omega_\S,\omega_\J$ respectively.
    Indeed we see that $\pi_\S\circ\iota_\S=\id$ and $\pi_\J\circ\iota_\J=\id$.
    And $\iota_\S\circ\pi_\S$ maps $(\omega,J)$ to $(\omega,J_{g,\omega})$ which is homotopic to the identity via
    $$ [0,1]\times\C(V) \to \C(V),\qquad (t,J,\omega) \mapsto (\omega,J_{tg+(1-t)\omega(\bul,J\bul),\omega}) , $$
    and $\iota_\J\circ\pi_\J$ maps $(\omega,J)$ to $(\omega_{g,J},J)$ which is homotopic to the identity via
    $$ [0,1]\times\C(V) \to \C(V),\qquad (t,J,\omega) \mapsto (t\omega_{g,J}+(1-t)\omega,J) . $$

\eremark

\bremark

    We can understand the above remark by focusing on the case $V=\bR^{2n}$ with the standard symplectic and complex structures.
    We then get the following diagram

    \diagram{

        \J(V,g)&\J(V)&\C(V)&\S(V)&\S(V,g)
    }{
        \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={1,2}, left cap={(}}
        \diagarrow{from={2,1}, to={2,2}, left cap={(}}
        \diagarrow{from={1,5}, to={1,4}, left cap={(^}}
        \diagarrow{from={2,5}, to={2,4}, left cap={(^}}
        \diagarrow{from={1,3}, to={1,2}}
        \diagarrow{from={2,3}, to={2,2}, text={\pi_\J}, y distance=.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,3}, to={1,4}}
        \diagarrow{from={2,3}, to={2,4}, text={\pi_\S}, y distance=.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={2,1}, text=\sim, x distance=.15cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,2}, to={2,2}, text=\sim, x distance=.15cm}

    The horizontal arrows are the homotopy equivalences discussed thus far, and the vertical arrows are diffeomorphisms.
    The outer vertical arrows are obtained by quotienting $\Psi\mapsto\Psi^*J_0$ or $\Psi^*\omega_0$ and quotienting.

    The inner vertical arrow is defined as follows.
    Recall that by \refmath[proposition]{compatcompstruct} $(\omega,J)\in\C(V)$ iff there is a linear isomorphism $\Psi\colon\bR^{2n}\to V$ such that $\Psi^*\omega_0=\omega_0$ and $\Psi J_0\Psi^{-1}=J$.
    So define the surjection
    $$ \gl_{2n}(\bR) \to \C(\bR^{2n}),\qquad \Psi \mapsto \Psi^*(\omega_0,J_0) = (\Psi^*\omega_0,\Psi J_0\Psi^{-1}) . $$
    The kernel is $\Sp(2n)\cap\gl_n(\bC)=\unitary(n)$, giving the desired diffeomorphism.

\eremark

\bdefn

    Let $(V,\omega)$ be a symplectic space.
    Then a complex linear structure $J\in\J(V)$ is called {\emphcolor $\omega$-tame} if
    $$ \omega(v,Jv) > 0 \hbox{ for all $v\neq0$} . $$
    Denote the space of $\omega$-tame complex structures by $\J_\tau(V,\omega)$.

\edefn

Note that $\J_\tau(V,\omega)\subseteq\J(V,\omega)$ is open, and for $J\in\J_\tau(V,\omega)$,
$$ g_{\omega,J}(v,u) = \frac12(\omega(v,Ju) - \omega(Jv,u)) $$
is an inner product.

\bprop

    The space $\J_\tau(V,\omega)$ is contractible.

\eprop

\bproof

    Define the space of tame pairs
    $$ \C_\tau(V) = \set{(\omega,J)}[\omega\in\S(V),\ J\in\J_\tau(V,\omega)] . $$
    Now for $(\omega,J)\in\C_\tau(V)$ define the $2$-form
    $$ \pi_1(\omega,J) = \frac12(\omega + J^*\omega) . $$
    Then
    $$ \frac12(\omega + J^*\omega)(\bul,J\bul) = \frac12(\omega(\bul,J\bul) - \omega(J\bul,\bul)) = g_{\omega,J} , $$
    which means that $\pi_1(\omega,J)$ is non-degenerate and thus symplectic.
    Furthermore $\pi_1(\omega,J)$ is compatible with $J$ and defines the same inner product as $\omega$, $g_{\omega,J}$.

    The fiber of the projection
    $$ \proj\colon\C_\tau(V) \to \C(V),\qquad (\omega,J) \mapsto \parens{\frac12(\omega+J^*\omega),J} $$
    over $(\omega,J)\in\C(V)$ consists of all $(\omega+\tau,J)\in\C_\tau(V)$ for $\tau\in\Lambda^2(V^*)$ such that $\tau+J^*\tau=0$.
    Any such pair is tame, as
    $$ \tau(v,Jv) = -\tau(Jv,J^2v) = \tau(v,Jv) \implies \tau(v,Jv) = 0 $$
    and thus $(\omega+\tau)(v,Jv)>0$ as $\omega$ is compatible.

    Note that the set of all $2$-forms $\tau$ such that $\tau+J^*\tau=0$ is a vector space.
    Thus we see that $\proj\colon\C_\tau(V)\to\C(V)$ is a vector bundle, and thus is a homotopy equivalence.
    By \refmath[remark]{compatpairs} so is $\pi_\S\colon\C(V)\to\S(V)$, and their composition $\pi_\S\circ\proj\colon\C_\tau(V)\to\S(V)$ is the map $\pi_1$.

    Define
    $$ \pi_0\colon\C_\tau(V) \to \S(V),\qquad \pi_0(\omega,J) = \omega $$
    and consider the diagram

    \diagram{
        \C_\tau(V)&\C(V)\cr
        \S(V)&\S(V)
    }{
        \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={1,2}, text=\proj, y distance=.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={2,1}, text=\pi_0, x distance=-.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,1}, to={2,2}, text=\pi_1, x distance=.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={1,2}, to={2,2}, text=\pi_\S, x distance=.2cm}
        \diagarrow{from={2,1}, to={2,2}, ds, right cap=-}
    }

    The top triangle commutes, but the bottom does not.
    Despite this, $\pi_1$ and $\pi_0$ are homotopic via $\pi_t=(1-t)\pi_0+t\pi_1$: because both $\pi_0(\omega,J)$ and $\pi_1(\omega,J)$ are compatible with $J$, their convex combinations are symplectic.
    Thus $\pi_0\colon\C_\tau(V)\to\S(V)$ is a homotopy equivalence, and as it is a fibration, its fibers $\pi_0^{-1}\omega\cong\J_\tau(V,\omega)$ are contractible.
    \qed

\eproof

We now construct a smooth map
$$ \Met(V) \times \S(V) \times \Lambda^2(V^*) \to \endo(V),\qquad (g,\omega,\hat\omega) \mapsto A_g(\omega,\hat\omega) $$
which has the following properties:
\benum
    \item $\hat\omega+\omega(A_g(\omega,\hat\omega)\bul,\bul)+\omega(\bul,A_g(\omega,\hat\omega)\bul)=0$,
    \item For every $B\in\endo(V)$: if $\omega(B\bul,\bul)+\omega(\bul,B\bul)=0$ then
    $$ \iprod{A_g(\omega,\hat\omega),B}_g = 0 , $$
    where $\iprod{\bul,\bul}_g$ is the induced inner product on $\endo(V)$.
    \item If $\Phi\colon V'\to V$ is an isomorphism,
    $$ A_{\Phi^*g}(\Phi^*\omega,\Phi^*\hat\omega) = \Phi^{-1}A_g(\omega,\hat\omega)\Phi . $$
\eenum
The conditions (1) and (2) uniquely define $A_g$.
Indeed, consider the map
$$ F\colon\endo(V) \to \Lambda^2(V^*),\qquad F(A) = \omega(A\bul,\bul) + \omega(\bul,A\bul) . $$
The kernel of this map is $\ker F=\lsp(V,\omega)$, which by \refmath[proposition]{eucsplie} has dimension $2n^2+n$.
And so we have that
$$ \dim\ker F + \dim\Lambda^2(V^*) = 2n^2 + n + 2n^2 - n = (2n)^2 = \dim\endo(V) , $$
meaning $F$ is surjective.
Thus there are $A\in\endo(V)$ such that $F(A)=-\hat\omega$, i.e.\ satisfying (1).

Condition (2) requires that $A$ be orthogonal to $\ker F$.
In general if we have a direct sum $V=U\oplus W$, then for every $v\in V$, the intersection $(v+U)\cap W$ is a singleton.
This is because the canonical map $W\to V/U$ is an isomorphism, so every coset $v+U\in V/U$ has a single element of $W$.
In this case we consider $\endo(V)=\ker F\oplus(\ker F)^\perp$, then $F^{-1}(-\hat\omega)$ is an affine subset of the form $A+\ker F$, and thus has a unique intersection
with $(\ker F)^\perp$; this gives us the unique $A_g(\omega,\hat\omega)$.

Point (3) follows directly from the existence and uniqueness imposed by (1) and (2).

We also construct a smooth map
$$ \Met(V)\times[0,1]\times\C_\tau(V)\to\gl(V),\qquad (g,t,\omega,J) \mapsto \Psi_g(t,\omega,J) $$
satisfying the following:
\benum
    \item $\Psi_g(0,\omega,J)=\id_V$.
    \item If $\omega=J^*\omega$ (i.e.\ $(\omega,J)\in\C(V)$) then $\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)=\id_V$.
    \item For all $g,t,\omega,J$,
    $$ \Psi_g(t,\omega,J)^*\parens{\omega + \frac t2(J^*\omega-\omega)} = \omega . $$
    \item For any isomorphism $\Phi\colon V'\to V$,
    $$ \Psi_{\Phi^*g}(t,\Phi^*\omega,\Phi^*J) = \Phi^{-1}\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)\Phi . $$
\eenum

For $g\in\Met(V),\omega\in\S(V),J\in\J_\tau(V,\omega)$, define $t\mapsto\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)$ to be the unique solution of the ODE
$$ \drv t\Psi_g(t,\omega,J) = A_g\parens{\omega + \frac t2(J^*\omega-\omega),\frac12(J^*\omega-\omega)}\Psi_g(t,\omega,J) , $$
with the initial condition $\Psi_g(0,\omega,J)=\id$.
Abbreviate
$$ \omega_t = \omega + \frac t2(J^*\omega-\omega),\qquad \hat\omega = \frac12(J^*\omega-\omega),\qquad \Psi_t = \Psi_g(t,\omega,J),\qquad A_t = A_g(\omega_t,\hat\omega) . $$
Then the ODE becomes
$$ \partial_t\Psi_t = A_t\Psi_t,\qquad \Psi_0 = \id,\qquad \partial_t\omega = \hat\omega . $$
Thus
$$ \eqalign{
    \drv t\Psi_t^*\omega_t &= \Psi_t^*\partial_t\omega_t + \omega_t(\partial_t\Psi_t\bul,\Psi_t\bul) + \omega_t(\Psi_t\bul,\partial_t\Psi_t\bul)\cr
        &= \Psi_t^*\hat\omega + \omega_t(A_t\Psi_t\bul,\bul) + \omega_t(\Psi_t\bul,A_t\Psi_t\bul)\cr
        &= \Psi_t^*(\hat\omega + \omega_t(A_g(\omega_t,\hat\omega)\bul,\bul) + \omega_t(\bul,A_g(\omega_t,\hat\omega)\bul))\cr
        &= 0.\cr
} $$
Thus we see $\Psi_t^*\omega_t$ is constant, and thus equal to $\Psi_0^*\omega_0=\omega$.
Thus $\Psi_t$ satisfies (3).

When $\omega=J^*\omega$ then $\omega_t=\omega$ and $\hat\omega=0$, thus $A_t=A_g(\omega,0)=0$ (because $A_g(\omega,0)$ is in the kernel of the map defined above,
but also orthogonal to it, thus it must be zero).
And so $\Psi_t$ is constant, and so equal to $\id$, showing (2).

Equivariance, point (4), follows from equivariance of $A_g$ and uniqueness of solutions to ODEs.
Smoothness follows from smoothness of solutions to ODEs.

These definitions allow us to define a homotopy inverse to $\J(V,\omega)\inj\J_\tau(V,\omega)$ by choosing an inner product $g$ and defining
$$ \J_\tau(V,\omega) \to \J(V,\omega),\qquad J\mapsto \Psi_g(1,\omega,J)^{-1}J\Psi_g(1,\omega,J) . $$
Now define
$$ f_\bul\colon[0,1]\times\J_\tau(V,\omega) \to \J_\tau(V,\omega),\quad f_t(J) = \Psi_g(t,\omega,J)^*J,\qquad \omega_t = \omega + \frac t2(J^*\omega-\omega) . $$
Because $\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)^*\omega_t=\omega$ and $J\in\J_\tau(V,\omega_t)$, we have that $f_t(J)=\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)^*J\in\J_\tau(V,\omega)$ for all $t$, so this is well-defined.
If $J\in\J(V,\omega)$ then $\Psi_g(t,\omega,J)=\id$ and so $f_t(J)=J$.
And for $t=1$, $J$ is compatible with $\omega_1$ so $f_1(\bul)\in\J(V,\omega)$.

Thus $f_\bul$ forms a strong homotopy retract $\J(V,\omega)\inj\J_\tau(V,\omega)$, so they are homotopy equivalent, and thus $\J_\tau(V,\omega)$ is contractible.
This gives an alternative proof of the above proposition.

\bprop[name={sympfamfromiso}]

    Let $\omega_t$ be a family of symplectic forms on $V$, depending smoothly on $t\in\bR$.
    Then there exists a smooth family of isomorphisms $\Phi_t\colon V\to V$ such that $\Phi_0=\id$ and $\Phi_t^*\omega_t=\omega_0$ for all $t$.

\eprop

\bproof

    Fix $g\in\Met(V)$ and define, similar to above,
    $$ \Psi_t = \Psi_g(t,\omega_0,J),\qquad A_t = A_g(\omega_t,\hat\omega),\qquad \hat\omega = \partial_t\omega . $$
    Then let $\Psi_t$ be the unique solution to the ODE with initial condition
    $$ \partial_t\Psi_t = A_t\Psi_t,\qquad \Psi_0 = \id . $$
    The same computation as above shows that $\partial_t\Psi_t^*\omega_t=0$ and thus $\Psi_t^*\omega_t=\omega_0$ for all $t$.
    \qed

\eproof
