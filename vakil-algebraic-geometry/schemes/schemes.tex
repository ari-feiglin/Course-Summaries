\subsection{Schemes}

Note that when $\pi\colon X\to Y$ is a homeomorphism, there is an isomorphism of categories $\pi\colon\open(Y)\cong\open(X)$ mapping $V\mapsto\pi^{-1}V$.
This lifts to an isomorphism of categories $\pi_*\colon\C_X^\pre\cong\C_Y^\pre$ mapping a presheaf $\F\in\C_X^\pre$ to precomposition with $\pi$: $\pi_*\F(V)=\F(\pi^{-1}V)$.
This is the usual pushforward, and it then induces an isomorphism $\C_X\cong\C_Y$.

Because $\pi^{-1}$ is its left adjoint, which is unique up to isomorphism, this means that $\pi^{-1},\pi_*$ form an adjoint equivalence.
In particular, the adjunction preserves categorical properties of morphisms, e.g.\ a morphism $\F\to\pi_*\G$ is an isomorphism iff $\pi^{-1}\F\to\G$ is.

\bdefn

    Let $\C$ be a category (with enough limits and colimits).
    A {\emphcolor $\C$-space} is a pair $(X,\O_X)$ where $X$ is a topological space and $\O_X$ is a $\C$-valued sheaf on $X$.
    A morphism of $\C$-spaces $(X,\O_X)\to(Y,\O_Y)$ is a continuous map $\pi\colon X\to Y$ along with a map of sheaves $\phi\colon\O_Y\to\pi_*\O_X$.
    Composition is given in the obvious manner: if $X\xtos\pi Y\xtos\tau Z$ are continuous, and $\phi\colon\O_Y\to\pi_*\O_X$ and $\psi\colon\O_Z\to\tau_*\O_Y$ are morphisms, then define
    $$ (\tau,\psi)\circ(\pi,\phi) = \O_Z \xtos\psi \tau_*\O_Y \xtos{\tau_*\phi} (\tau\circ\pi)_*\O_X . $$
    We denote the category of $\C$-spaces by $\Space_\C$.

\edefn

Note that composition is indeed associative.
If we have maps
$$ (X,\O_X) \xtos{(\pi,\phi)} (Y,\O_Y) \xtos{(\tau,\psi)} (Z,\O_Z) \xtos{(\sigma,\chi)} (W,\O_W) $$
then we have compositions
$$ \displaylines{
    \bigl((\sigma,\chi)\circ(\tau,\psi)\bigr)\circ(\pi,\phi) = (\sigma\circ\tau,\sigma_*\psi\circ\chi)\circ(\pi,\phi) = (\sigma\circ\tau\circ\pi,(\sigma\circ\tau)_*\phi\circ\sigma_*\psi\circ\chi\cr
    (\sigma,\chi)\circ\bigl((\tau,\psi)\circ(\pi,\phi)\bigr) = (\sigma,\chi)\circ(\tau\circ\pi,\tau_*\phi\circ\psi) = (\sigma\circ\tau\circ\pi,\sigma_*(\tau_*\phi\circ\psi)\circ\chi
} $$
which are equal.

Note that by the adjunction $\pi^{-1}\dashv\pi_*$, to give a morphism $\O_Y\to\pi_*\O_X$ is to give a morphism $\pi^{-1}\O_Y\to\O_X$.
In which case the composition of $\phi\colon\pi^{-1}\O_Y\to\O_X$ and $\psi\colon\tau^{-1}\O_Z\to\O_Y$ is
$$ (\tau,\psi)\circ(\pi,\phi) \colon (\tau\circ\pi)^{-1}\O_Z \xtos{\pi^{-1}\psi} \pi^{-1}\O_Y \xtos\phi \O_X . $$

\bdefn[title={Schemes}]

    A {\emphcolor ringed space} is a $\Ring$-space; we write $\RS$ for $\Space_\Ring$.

    An {\emphcolor affine scheme} is a ringed space which is isomorphic to $(\spec A,\O_{\spec A})$ for some ring $A$.
    A {\emphcolor scheme} is a ringed space $(X,\O_X)$ which is locally affine: for every $p\in X$ there is an open neighborhood $p\in U$ such that $(U,\O_X|_U)$ is affine.
    The topology of a scheme is called the {\emphcolor Zariski topology}.

\edefn

Schemes are ringed spaces, but we will see that not all morphisms of ringed spaces should be considered morphisms of schemes.
Though we will see that an isomorphism of schemes as ringed spaces gives an isomorphism of rings (when we define what
morphisms of schemes are).

We can define the {\emphcolor global sections functor}
$$ \Gamma\colon\Space_\C^\op\to\C,\qquad \Gamma(X,\O_X)=\O_X(X) . $$
A morphism $(\pi,\phi)\colon(X,\O_X)\to(Y,\O_Y)$ determines a map $\phi\colon\O_Y\to\pi_*\O_X$, which has component $\phi_Y\colon\O_Y(Y)\to\O_X(\pi^{-1}Y)=\O_X(X)$.
Thus we define $\Gamma(\pi,\phi)=\phi_Y$, turning $\Gamma$ into a functor.

If $X$ is a topological space and $U\subseteq X$ an open set, we may write $\Gamma(U,\O_X)$ instead of $\Gamma(U,\O_X|_U)$.
In general we may write $(U,\O_X)$ instead of $(U,\O_X|_U)$.

\bnote

    Note that the global sections functor on sheaves is covariant, while the global sections functor on $\C$-spaces is contravariant.

\enote

\blemm

    There is a natural isomorphism
    $$ (\dof(f),\O_{\spec A}) \cong (\spec A_f,\O_{\spec A_f}) . $$

\elemm

\bproof

    Consider the canonical map $\iota\colon A\to A_f$, which induces the continuous injective $\iota\colon\spec(A_f)\to\spec(A)$.
    Its image consists of all prime ideals not intersecting $\set{f^k}_{k\geq0}$, equivalently not containing $f$, so $\dof(f)$.
    It is also an open map: $\dof_{A_f}(g/f^n)$ contains all prime ideals of $A_f$ not containing $g/f^n$; since $f^n$ is a unit, this is just all prime ideals not containing $g$.
    Thus $\iota\dof_{A_f}(g/f^n)=\dof(g)\cap\dof(f)=\dof(fg)$ which is open.
    Thus $\iota$ forms a homeomorphism $\spec(A_f)\xtos\sim\dof(f)$.

    Now we need to show that there is an isomorphism $\O_{\spec A}|_{\dof f}\cong\iota_*\O_{\spec A_f}$.
    Note that for $\dof(g)\subseteq\dof(f)$ we have
    $$ \O_{\spec A}(\dof g)=A_g,\qquad \iota_*\O_{\spec A_f}(\dof g)=\O_{\spec A_f}(\iota^{-1}\dof g)=\O_{\spec A_f}(\dof g) = (A_f)_g \cong A_g . $$
    So there is indeed an isomorphism, as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bcoro

    If $(X,\O_X)$ is a scheme and $U\subseteq X$ is open, then $(U,\O_X|_U)$ is a scheme.

\ecoro

\bproof

    For an affine scheme this is due to the previous lemma: because the $\dof(f)$ form a base, for any $p\in U$ there is an $f\in A$ such that $p\in\dof(f)\subseteq U$.
    Then $(\dof(f),\O_X|_{\dof f})\subseteq(U,\O_X|_U)$ is an open affine subscheme.

    In general let $p\in U$, then take $p\in V\subseteq X$ an affine neighborhood.
    Then $p\in U\cap V\subseteq U$ is an open subset of an affine scheme and thus there is an affine neighborhood $p\in W\subseteq U\cap V$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bdefn

    If $(X,\O_X)$ is a scheme and $U\subseteq X$ open, then $(U,\O_X|_U)$ is called an {\emphcolor open subscheme} of $X$.
    If $U$ is also an affine scheme, then $U$ is an {\emphcolor affine open subscheme} or an {\emphcolor affine open subset}.

\edefn

It is clear that for a scheme $X$, its affine open subsets form a base for the topology.

\bdefn

    Let $\C$ be a category, a subcategory $\C_0\subseteq\C$ is said to be {\emphcolor replete} if it is closed under isomorphisms: if $c\in\C_0$ and $f\colon c\xto\sim d$ is an isomorphism
    then both $d$ and $f$ are in $\C_0$.

    Let $\C_0\subseteq\C$ be a replete category.
    A $(\C,\C_0)$-space is a $\C$-space $(X,\O_X)$ such that for every $p\in X$, the stalk $\O_{X,p}$ is in $\C_0$.
    A morphism of $(\C,\C_0)$-spaces is a $\C$-space morphism $(\pi,\phi)\colon(X,\O_X)\to(Y,\O_Y)$ such that for every $p\in X$ the morphism of stalks $\phi_p\colon(\pi^{-1}\O_Y)_p\cong\O_{Y,\pi(p)}\to\O_{X,p}$
    is in $\C_0$.

\edefn

\bnote

    We can write $\phi_p\colon\O_{Y,\pi p}\to\O_{X,p}$ more explicitly.
    For $\pi(p)\in V\subseteq Y$ open we have a map
    $$ \phi_V\colon\O_Y(V)\to\O_X(\pi^{-1}V)\to\colim_{p\in U}\O_X(U) = \O_{X,p} . $$
    These then assemble into a cone $\O_Y(\bul)\To\O_{X,p}$ which then limits to $\O_{Y,\pi(p)}\to\O_{X,p}$.

\enote

\bdefn

    Define $\LocRing$ to be the category of local rings $(A,\m)$.
    A morphism $\phi\colon(A,\m)\to(B,\n)$ is a ring morphism $A\to B$ such that $\phi(\m)\subseteq\n$.

    A {\emphcolor locally ringed space} is a $(\Ring,\LocRing)$-space.
    We denote the category of locally ringed spaces by $\LRS$.

\edefn

\bnote

    Importantly, a locally ringed space is not a $\LocRing$-space.
    In fact, generally a locally ringed space's sections will not be local rings.

\enote

A scheme which is isomorphic to the spectrum of a ring is called an {\emphcolor affine scheme}.
We denote the full subcategory of affine schemes by $\AffSch$.

Schemes are locally ringed spaces: if $X$ is a scheme and $p\in X$ then $p$ has an affine neighborhood.
Since stalks can be taken locally, it is sufficient to show that $\spec A$ is a locally ringed space for any ring $A$.
And this is clear as the stalk of $\spec A$ at $[\p]\in\spec A$ is isomorphic to $A_\p$ which is local.

\bdefn

    The {\emphcolor category of schemes} is the full subcategory of $\LocRing$ whose objects are schemes; it is denoted by
    $\Sch$.
    The full subcategory of $\Sch$ consisting of affine schemes is denoted $\AffSch$.

\edefn

Note that if $X,Y$ are locally ringed space which are isomorphic as ringed spaces, then they are isomorphic as locally ringed
spaces.
Indeed in general if $X,Y$ are $(\C,\C_0)$-spaces which are isomorphic as $\C$-spaces, they are isomorphic as
$(\C,\C_0)$-spaces.
This is because $\C_0$ is replete (contains all isomorphisms).

Note that $\spec$ forms a contravariant functor $\Ring^\op\to\AffSch$ which maps a ring $A$ to is spectrum.
Then for a ring morphism $\phi\colon A\to B$ we have a continuous map $\phi^*\colon\spec B\to\spec A$ mapping $\q\leq B$
to $\phi^{-1}\q$.
And $\phi^\sharp\colon\spec A\to \phi^*_*\spec B$ which for $f\in A$ maps
$\phi^\sharp_f\colon A_f\to\spec B((\phi^*)^{-1}\dof_B(f))=B_{\phi f}$.
This is just the natural map induced from the universal $A\xtos\phi B\to B_{\phi f}$.

This is clearly seen to be functorial.

\bdefn

    If $(X,\O_X)$ is a locally ringed space, then for $p\in X$ denote the maximal ideal of the stalk $\O_{X,p}$ by
    $\m_{X,p}$ or $\m_p$.
    The {\emphcolor residue field} is $\kappa(p)=\O_{X,p}/\m_p$.
    For a function $f$ on an open subset $U\subseteq X$ has a value at every $p\in U$ denote by $f(p)$: it is the image of
    the germ of $f$ at $p$ under the quotient $\O_{X,p}\to\kappa(p)$.
    We say that $f$ {\emphcolor vanishes} at $p\in X$ if $f(p)=0$.

\edefn

\bprop[name={sectionvanishingset}]

    If $f$ is a function on a locally ringed space $X$, then the subset of where $f$ vanishes is closed.
    Conversely if $f$ vanishes nowhere then it is invertible.

\eprop

\bproof

    Note that $X$ does not vanish at $p$ iff $f_p\notin\m_p$ iff $f_p$ is invertible in $\O_{X,p}$.
    For a ringed space $X$ the subset of points where $f$ is invertible in the stalk is open.
    Indeed, if $f$ is invertible in $\O_{X,p}$ then there is a $p\in U$ and a $g\in\O_X(U)$ such that $fg=1$ in $\O_{X,p}$
    so that $fg=1$ in a neighborhood around $p$; so $f$ is invertible in that neighborhood.

    Thus the set of points where $f$ is not invertible, for $X$ locally ringed $f(p)=0$, is closed.

    For the second point, at every $p\in X$ there is an open $g_p\in U_p$ which is the inverse of $f$ in $U_p$.
    Because inverses are unique, the $g_p$ agree on their overlaps, so we can glue them together to get a global inverse for
    $f$.
    \qed

\eproof

For an affine scheme $\spec A$ and a point $[\p]\in\spec A$, the stalk at $[\p]$ is isomorphic to $A_\p$.
This is local with maximal ideal $\p A_p$, so that $\kappa(\p)=A_\p/\p A_\p$.
Localization commutes with quotients, so this is isomorphic to the localization of $A/\p$ at the image of $\p$, which is the
zero ideal.
Thus
$$ \kappa(\p) = \frac{A_\p}{\p A_\p} = \Frac\parens{A/\p} . $$

\bexam

    Consider the scheme $\bA^2_k=\spec k[x,y]$, where $k$ is a field of characteristic not $2$.
    Then $(x^2+y^2)/x(y^2-x^5)$ is a function away from the $y$-axis and $y^2-x^5$ (i.e.\ it exists in $\dof(y,y^2-x^5)$).
    Its value at $(2,4)$, meaning $(x-2,y-4)$ is
    $$ \frac{x^2+y^2}{x(y^2-x^5)} \equiv \frac{2^2+4^2}{2(4^2-2^5)} . $$
    Its value at $(y)$, the generic point of the $x$-axis, is
    $$ \frac{x^2+y^2}{x(y^2-x^5)} \equiv \frac{x^2}{-x^6} = -\frac1{x^4} . $$

\eexam

\bremark

    We can define manifolds of arbitrary flavor similar to schemes.
    Instead of our base space being $\spec A$, we can choose different base spaces.
    For instance a topological manifold is a ringed space $(X,\O_X)$ which is locally isomorphic to an open ball in $\bR^n$
    with the sheaf of real-valued continuous functions on it.
    Similarly a $C^k$-manifold is a ringed space which is locally isomorphic to an open in $\bR^n$ with the sheaf of real-valued
    $C^k$ functions on it.
    And a complex manifold is a ringed space locally isomorphic to an open ball in $\bC^n$ with the sheaf of holomorphic
    complex-valued functions on it.

    In each of these examples we must also require that $X$ be second-countable and Hausdorff.

\eremark

\bthrm[name={specsectionequiv}]

    There is a contravariant equivalence of categories $\spec\colon\Ring^\op\to\AffSch\colon\Gamma$.

\ethrm

\bproof

    Because
    $$ \Gamma\spec A = \spec A(\dof 1) = A_1 = A $$
    naturally, it is sufficient to show that $\spec$ is fully faithful (as it is by definition essentially surjective).
    First we note that for a ring morphism $\phi\colon A\to B$, we can obtain $\phi$ from $\spec\phi\colon\spec B\to\spec A$
    as $\phi=\Gamma\spec\phi$.
    Indeed $\spec\phi$ acts on global sections by the canonical map $A=A_1\to B_{\phi1}=B$, which is $\phi$.
    Thus $\spec$ is faithful.

    Similarly if $\phi\colon\spec B\to\spec A$ is a morphism of schemes, then we claim $\phi=\spec\Gamma\phi$.
    Indeed, let $\phi_1=\Gamma\phi\colon A\to B$ and $\psi=\spec\phi_1$; further let $\phi$ denote the underlying topological
    map of $\phi$ and $\phi^\sharp\colon\O_{\spec A}\to\phi_*\O_{\spec B}$ be the sheaf morphism.
    By definition for $\q\in\spec B$, $\phi^\sharp_\q\colon\O_{\spec A,\phi\q}\to\O_{\spec B,\q}$ is local; meaning
    $$ (\phi^\sharp_\q)^{-1}(\m_\q) = \m_{\phi q} . $$
    Recalling $\O_{\spec A,\p}\cong A_\p$, we have the following commutative square

    \commsq{A&B\cr A_{\phi\q}&B_\q}
        {\phi_1}{\phi^\sharp_\q}{}{}

    The image of $a\in A$ in the diagonal is then $\phi^\sharp_\q(a/1)=\phi_1(a)/1$.
    Thus $\phi^\sharp_\q(a/1)\in\m_\q$ iff $\phi_1(a)\in\q$.
    The former occurs only if $a/1\in\m_{\phi\q}$ iff $a\in\phi\q$.
    So $a\in\phi\q$ iff $\phi_1(a)\in\q$, so that $\phi_1^{-1}\q=\phi\q$; meaning $\phi_1^*=\phi$ as continuous maps.

    Now we need to show that $\phi_1^\sharp=\phi^\sharp$ as sheaf maps.
    We do this on the distinguished open subsets.
    For $f\in A$ we have that
    $$ \phi^{-1}\dof_A(f) = (\phi_1^*)^{-1}\dof_A(f) = \set{\q\leq B}[f\notin\phi_1^{-1}\q] = \dof_B(\phi_1f) . $$
    And we want to show that
    $$ \phi^\sharp_f \colon \O_{\spec A}(\dof f) = A_f \to \O_{\spec B}(\phi^{-1}\dof f) = B_{\phi_1f} $$
    is equal to induced map $A_f\to B_{\phi_1f}$ given by $A\xtos{\phi_1}B\to B_{\phi_1f}$.
    But this immediate from the naturality of $\phi$: the following diagram commutes

    \commsq{A&B\cr A_f&B_{\phi_1f}}
        {\phi_1}{\phi^\sharp_f}{}{}

    Thus we get the desired result.
    \qed

\eproof

\bexam

    Let $A=k[x,y]$, so $\spec A=\bA^2_k$; define $U=\bA^2-\set{(0,0)}=\bA^2-\set{(x,y)}$.
    Note that $U=\dof(x)\cup\dof(y)$, which forms an affine cover so that $U$ is a scheme.
    We claim that $U$ is not affine; and thus not a distinguished open set.

    Indeed, inclusion $U\inj\spec A$ induces a map $\Gamma U\to A$.
    We claim it is an isomorphism: this is sufficient as the inclusion is not an isomorphism, and if $U$ were affine as $\Gamma$
    is an equivalence this would imply it were.

    Indeed, functions on $U$ are functions on $\dof(x)$ and $\dof(y)$ which agree on their intersection:
    $\dof(x)\cap\dof(y)=\dof(xy)$.
    I.e.\ rational maps in $A_x$ and $A_y$ which are in $A_{xy}$.
    These are just the polynomials so $U=k[x,y]=A$.

\eexam

\blemm

    The global sections functor $\Gamma\colon\LocRing^\op\to\Ring$ is fully faithful on morphisms from a locally ringed space to an affine scheme.
    That is for a locally ringed space $X$ and a ring $A$,
    $$ \LocRing(X,\spec A) \xtos\Gamma \Ring(A,\Gamma X) $$
    is bijective.

\elemm

\bproof

    Suppose we have two morphisms $\phi,\psi\colon X\to\spec A$ where $X$ is a locally ringed space, such that
    $\Gamma\phi=\Gamma\psi$.
    Let us write $\phi^\sharp$ for the sheaf morphism part of $\phi$, and similar for $\psi$; we use $\phi$ for the continuous
    part.
    Furthermore write $\phi_1$ for $\Gamma\phi$, similar for $\psi$.
    Let $p\in X$: we want to show that $\phi(p)=\psi(p)$.

    Let $\q_1=\phi(p)$ and $\q_2=\psi(p)$, and consider the following commutative square:

    \commsq{A&\Gamma X\cr A_{\q_1}&\O_{X,p}}
        {\phi_1}{\phi_p}{}\loc

    Because $\phi_p$ is a local morphism we have that for $a\in A$, $a\in\q_1$ iff $\phi_p(a/1)\in\m_{X,p}$.
    This is iff $\loc\circ\phi_1(a)\in\m_{X,p}$.
    Because $\phi_1=\psi_1$, this means that $a\in\q_1$ iff $a\in\q_2$ so that $\phi(p)=\psi(p)$ as desired.

    Now we want to show that $\phi^\sharp=\psi^\sharp$; we can do this on distinguished open sets.
    For $f\in A$ we have the commutative square

    \commsq{A&\O_X(X)\cr A_f&\O_X(\phi^{-1}\dof(f))}
        {\phi_1}{\phi_f}{}{}

    Note that by localization $\phi_f$ is the unique morphism making this square commute.
    Since $\phi_1=\psi_1$ this implies $\phi_f=\psi_f$.
    Thus $\Gamma$ is faithful.

    For fullness, suppose $\sigma\colon A\to\Gamma X$ is a ring morphism.
    For $p\in X$ let $\sigma_p\colon A\xtos\sigma\Gamma X\to\O_{X,p}$ be the composite and define
    $\phi(p)=\sigma_p^{-1}(\m_{X,p})$.
    The preimage of a maximal ideal is prime, so $\phi$ forms a map $X\to\spec A$.
    It is continuous: for $f\in A$:
    $$ \phi^{-1}\dof(f) = \set{p\in X}[\sigma_p(f)\notin\m_{X,p}] = \set{p\in X}[\sigma(f)(p)\neq0] , $$
    where $\sigma(f)(p)$ is the value of the global section $\sigma(f)$ at $p$.
    By \refmath[proposition]{sectionvanishingset} this is an open set.

    Now we define $\phi^\sharp\colon\spec A\to\phi_*X$ again on the distinguished open sets.
    For $f\in A$, $\sigma(f)$ vanishes nowhere in $\phi^{-1}\dof(f)$, so it is invertible in $\O_X(\phi^{-1}\dof f)$ again by
    \refmath[proposition]{sectionvanishingset}.
    Thus there is a unique map $\phi_f\colon A_f\to\O_X(\phi^{-1}\dof f)$ making the following square commute

    \commsq{A&\O_X(X)\cr A_f&\O_X(\phi^{-1}\dof f)}
        {\sigma}{\phi_f}{}{}

    This forms a morphism of sheaves, and $(\phi,\phi^\sharp)$ is a morphism of locally ringed spaces.
    For $p\in X$, let $\q=\phi(p)=\sigma_p^{-1}(\m_{X,p})$.
    We want to show that $\phi_p(\q A_\q)\subseteq\m_{X,p}$.
    By considering the following diagram

    \vfill\break

    \commsq{A&\O_X(X)\cr A_\q&\O_{X,p}}
        {\sigma}{\phi_p}{}{\loc}

    it is sufficient to show that $\loc\circ\sigma(\q)\subseteq\m_{X,p}$.
    By definition this is just $\sigma_p(\q)\subseteq\m_{X,p}$, which is clear by definition of $\q$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bthrm[name={specglobsectionadj}]

    The functors $\spec\colon\Ring^\op\to\LocRing$ and $\Gamma\colon\LocRing^\op\to\Ring$ are mutually right adjoint.
    That is, there is a natural isomorphism
    $$ \LocRing(X,\spec A) \cong \Ring(A,\Gamma X),\qquad A\in\Ring,\ X\in\LocRing . $$
    We can restrict this adjunction to $\Sch$ instead of $\LocRing$.

\ethrm

\bproof

    By the previous theorem, $\LocRing(X,\spec A)\xtos\Gamma\Ring(A,\Gamma X)$ is a bijection, thus a natural isomorphism.
    \qed

\eproof
