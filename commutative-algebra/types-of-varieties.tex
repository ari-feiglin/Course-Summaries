\section{Types of Varieties}

\subsection{Products of varieties}

The goal of this section is to construct the categorical product of varieties.
First we will construct the product of SWFs.

Recall that when constructing the colimits of SWFs we knew that the underlying topological space was the colimit of the spaces.
This is not true for limits; but we will see that the underlying set is the limit, but endowed with a different topology.

Let $(X,\O_X)$ and $(Y,\O_Y)$ be SWFs, then we construct a topology on $Z=X\times Y$ as follows.
For $U\subseteq X$ and $V\subseteq Y$ open, and $f\colon U\times V\to k$ of the form $f(x,y)=\sum_ig_i(x)h_i(y)$ for $g_i\in k[U]$
and $h_i\in k[V]$, define
$$ D_{U\times V}(f) = \set{(x,y)\in U\times V}[f(x,y)\neq0] . $$
This collection of $D_{U\times V}(f)$ forms a basis for our topology on $Z$.
Call such $f$ {\emphcolor basic regular} relative to $U,V$.

This allows us to define a structure sheaf on $Z$.
For $W\subseteq Z$ open, let $\O_Z(W)$ be the set of functions $\phi\colon W\to k$ such that there is a basic open cover
$W\subseteq\bigcup_iD_{U_i\times V_i}(f_i)$ and restricting $\phi$ to $W\cap D_{U_i\times V_i}(f_i)$ is of the form $\phi_i/f_i$
where $\phi_i$ is basic regular relative to $U,V$.

Notice that the projections $\pi_X\colon Z\to X$ and $\pi_Y\colon Z\to Y$ are morphisms of SWFs.
Indeed, $\pi_X^{-1}(D_U(f))=D_U(f)\times Y=D_{U\times Y}(f\cdot1)$ is open.
Since $D_U(f)$ forms a basis of open sets of $X$, we see that $\pi_X$ is continuous.
And for $f\colon U\to k$ regular, $\pi^*_X(f)(x,y)=f(x)$ is regular as well since $1\in k[Y]$.

Let us denote $(Z,\O_Z)$ by $(X,\O_X)\times(Y,\O_Y)$, or just $X\times Y$.

\blemm

    $(Z,\O_Z)$ is the categorical product of $(X,\O_X)$ and $(Y,\O_Y)$ in $\SWF_k$.

\elemm

\bproof

    For $\phi_X\colon T\to X$ and $\phi_Y\colon T\to Y$, in order for this to factor through $\phi\colon T\to Z$ we must have that
    $\phi=(\phi_X,\phi_Y)$ (since $Z$ is the product of sets).
    It remains to show that $\phi$ is a morphism.

    For every open $U\subseteq X,V\subseteq Y$, we have $\phi^{-1}(U\times V)=\phi_X^{-1}(U)\cap\phi^{-1}_Y(V)$ is open.
    And for every $f\colon U\times V\to k$ basic regular of the form $f(x,y)=\sum_ig_i(x)h_i(y)$, we have
    $$ \phi^*(f) = \sum_i\phi^*_X(g_i)\phi^*_Y(h_i) \colon \phi^{-1}(U\times V) \to k $$
    which is regular as $\phi_X,\phi_Y$ are morphisms and $k[\phi^{-1}(U\times V)]$ is an algebra.

    Thus we have that
    $$ \phi^{-1}(D_{U\times V}(f)) = D_{\phi^{-1}(U\times V)}(\phi^*(f)) \subseteq T $$
    is open, so $\phi$ is continuous.

    Now we need to show that $\phi^*$ induces a map of $k$-algebras.
    But this is immediate, as it maps basic regular maps to regular maps, and thus quotients of basic regular to regular maps.
    And the rest follows by the sheaf condition.
    \qed

\eproof

Since limits are functorial, if we have morphisms $f_i\colon X_i\to Y_i$ for $i=1,2$, the product $f_1\times f_2\colon X_1\times X_2\to Y_1\times Y_2$
is a morphism as well.

\blemm

    Let $(X,\O_X)$ and $(Y,\O_Y)$ be SWFs and $X'\subseteq X,Y'\subseteq Y$ be subsets.
    Then the following two SWFs on $X'\times Y'$ are the same
    $$ (X',\O_X|_{X'}) \times (Y',\O_Y|_{Y'}) = \bigl((X,\O_X)\times(Y,\O_Y)\bigr)|_{X'\times Y'} . $$

\elemm

\bproof

    Let $Z_1,Z_2$ denote the SWFs; we want to show $Z_1=Z_2$.
    We do this by showing that the identity of $X'\times Y'$ gives a morphism in both directions.

    In one direction, $\id\colon Z_1\to Z_2$ is a morphism since it is the restriction of the embedding $X'\times Y'\inj X\times Y$.
    This is just the product of the inclusions $(X',\O_X|_{X'})\to(X,\O_X)$ with the same one for $Y$, which is a morphism.

    Conversely we show by the universal property that $(X\times Y)|_{X'\times Y'}\to X$ obtained by projecting is a morphism.
    But this is just the restriction of the projection $X\times Y\to X$, which is a morphism.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    For every two sets $X,Y$, there is a natural injective homomorphism
    $$ \mu\colon \fun(X,k) \otimes_k \fun(Y,k) \to \fun(X\times Y,k),\qquad \mu(f\otimes g)\colon(x,y)\mapsto f(x)g(y) . $$

\elemm

\bproof

    $\mu$ is clearly bilinear, so it is well-defined.
    Since $\fun(X,k),\fun(Y,k)$ are $k$-vector spaces, to show injectivity it is sufficient to show that $\mu$ maps a basis to a linearly
    independent set.
    Take a basis $\set{f_i}_{i\in I}$ of $\fun(X,k)$ and a basis $\set{g_j}_{j\in J}$ of $\fun(Y,k)$.
    Then $\set{f_i\otimes g_j}_{i\in I,j\in J}$ is a basis for their tensor product, and $\mu$ maps this to $f_ig_j$.
    Suppose we have a finite zeroing sum
    $$ \sum_{i,j}\alpha_{ij}f_ig_j = 0 . $$
    Then for every $y\in Y$ we have the zeroing sum
    $$ \sum_i\sum_j\alpha_{ij}g_j(y)f_i = 0 , $$
    which means that for all $i$, $\sum_j\alpha_{ij}g_j(y)=0$ by linear independence.
    Since this is true for all $y$ this means $\sum_j\alpha_{ij}g_j=0$, and thus $\alpha_{ij}=0$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bthrm

    If $X,Y$ are affine varieties, then $X\times Y$ is an affine variety.
    Furthermore, the natural map $k[X]\otimes_kk[Y]\to k[X\times Y]$, $f\otimes g\mapsto\pi_X^*(f)\cdot\pi_Y^*(g)$ is an isomorphism.

    If $X,Y$ are algebraic varieties, then so is $X\times Y$.

\ethrm

\bproof

    We first prove the first point, where $X,Y$ are affine.
    Since $k[X],k[Y]$ are fg $k$-algebras, so is $k[X]\otimes_kk[Y]$.
    Furthermore, $k[X]\subseteq\fun(X,k)$ and $k[Y]\subseteq\fun(Y,k)$, and as $k$ is a field all $k$-modules are free and thus flat so tensoring
    preserves subobjects, thus $k[X]\otimes_kk[Y]\subseteq\fun(X,k)\otimes_k\fun(Y,k)$.
    By the previous lemma this is a subalgebra of $\fun(X\times Y,k)$ which is reduced.
    Thus $k[X]\otimes_kk[Y]$ is a reduced fg $k$-algebra.

    This means there is an affine $k$-variety $Z$ such that $k[Z]=k[X]\otimes_kk[Y]$.
    Given any other SWF, we have natural bijections
    $$ \eqalign{
        \hom_\SWF(W,Z) &\cong \hom_\alg(k[Z],\O_W(W))\cr
            &\cong \hom_\alg(k[X]\otimes_kk[Y],\O_W(W))\cr
            &\cong \hom_\alg(k[X],\O_W(W)) \times \hom_\alg(k[Y],\O_W(W))\cr
            &\cong \hom_\SWF(W,X) \times \hom_\SWF(W,Y)
    } $$
    where the first is due to a result we showed in general: the global sections functor $\Gamma$ is a natural isomorphism for $Z$ affine.
    The third is because tensoring over $k$ is the coproduct of $k$-algebras.
    Thus $Z$ satisfies the universal property of products, so $Z\cong X\times Y$ is affine.

    Alternatively, we could note that $\bA^n\times\bA^m\cong\bA^{n+m}$ via the identity.
    Then for $X\subseteq\bA^n,Y\subseteq\bA^m$, $X\times Y\subseteq\bA^{n+m}$ is closed as it is equal to $Z(\I(X)\cup\I(Y))$.
    Thus it is affine.

    Now in general, let $X=\bigcup_iX_i$ and $Y=\bigcup_jY_j$ be open affine coverings.
    Then $X\times Y=\bigcup_{i,j}X_i\times Y_j$ is an open affine cover by the first point, so $X\times Y$ is algebraic.
    \qed

\eproof

Note that since $A\otimes_kk[x_1,\dots,x_n]\cong A[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ naturally, we see that
$$ k[X\times\bA^n] \cong k[X]\otimes_kk[\bA^n] \cong k[X][x_1,\dots,x_n] $$
for $X$ affine.

\bcoro

    Let $X,Y$ be algebraic varieties, $X'\subseteq X,Y'\subseteq Y$ be open/closed subvarieties.
    Then $X'\times Y'\subseteq X\times Y$ is an open/closed subvariety.

\ecoro

\bproof

    $X'\times Y'$ is open/closed in $X\times Y$ and $X'\times Y'$ is a variety.
    Furthermore we showed that the induced SWF structure on $X'\times Y'$ from $X\times Y$ is the same as the product of induced structures
    from $X,Y$.
    Thus $X'\times Y'$ is a subvariety of $X\times Y$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bprop

    Let $X,Y$ be algebraic varieties, then
    \benum
        \item $\dim(X\times Y)=\dim X+\dim Y$,
        \item If $X$ and $Y$ are irrreducible, so is $X\times Y$.
    \eenum

\eprop

\bproof

    First we show (2): if $X,Y$ are irreducible varieties, then so is $X\times Y$.
    Suppose $X\times Y=Z_1\cup Z_2$ for $Z_i$ closed, then define
    $$ X_i = \set{x\in X}[x\times Y\subseteq Z_i] = \bigcap_{y\in Y}\set{x\in X}[(x,y)\in Z_i]. $$
    Notice that $X=X_1\cup X_2$, and $X_i$ are closed as each $\set{x\in X}[(x,y)\in Z_i]$ is closed as the preimage of $Z_i$
    under $x\mapsto(x,y)$.
    Thus $X=X_i$ for some $i$, thus $X\times Y\subseteq Z_i$ and we have the desired result.

    Note that the map $x\mapsto(x,y)$ is continuous as its components are the identity and the constant map, both of which are continuous.
    So it is even a morphism of SWFs.

    Now we claim that if $X,Y$ are affine then $\dim(X\times Y)=\dim X+\dim Y$.
    Let $\dim X=n,\dim Y=m$, then there are finite surjective maps $X\to\bA^n,Y\to\bA^m$.
    The product map $X\times Y\to\bA^{n+m}$ is then finite surjective, and thus $\dim(X\times Y)=\dim(\bA^{n+m})=n+m$ as desired.

    Then in general for $X=\bigcup_iX_i,Y=\bigcup_jY_j$ open affine covers, $X\times Y=\bigcup_{i,j}X_i\times Y_j$.
    Then we have
    $$ \dim(X\times Y) = \max_{i,j}\dim(X_i\times Y_j) = \max_{i,j}(\dim X_i+\dim Y_j) = \max_i\dim X_i + \max_j\dim Y_j = \dim X + \dim Y $$
    as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bthrm

    The product $\bP^n\times\bP^m$ is projective.

\ethrm

\bproof

    Define
    $$ S\colon\bP^n\times\bP^m\to\bP^{(n+1)(m+1)-1},\qquad (\ell_1,\ell_2)\mapsto\ell_1\otimes\ell_2 . $$
    Explicitly, this maps lines $\ell_1\subseteq k^{n+1}$ and $\ell_2\subseteq k^{m+1}$ to $\ell_1\otimes_k\ell_2\subseteq k^{(n+1)(m+1)}$
    which we then project down to $\bP^{(n+1)(m+1)-1}$.
    Alternatively $S$ maps $[x_0:\cdots:x_n]$ and $[y_0:\cdots:y_m]$ to
    $$ [z_{ij}]_{0\leq i\leq n,0\leq j\leq m},\qquad z_{ij} = x_i\cdot y_j . $$

    We claim that $S$ is a closed embedding, so $\im S$ is closed and $S$ induces an isomorphism $S\colon\bP^n\times\bP^m\xto\sim\im S$.
    Being a morphism, and even a closed embedding, is local on the target.

    So take the standard affine open covering $D(Z_{ij})\subseteq\bP^{(n+1)(m+1)-1}$, then
    $$ S^{-1}D(Z_{ij}) = \set{(X,Y)}[X_iY_j\neq0] = D(X_i) \times D(Y_j) \subseteq \bP^n \times \bP^m . $$
    So we need to show that $D(X_i)\times D(Y_j)\to D(Z_{ij})$ is a closed embedding.

    Both the source and target are affine varieties, so this is a closed embedding iff $S^*$ is a surjective morphism of coordinate rings.
    Identifying $D(X_i)\cong\bA^n$ by $[X_0:\cdots:X_n]\mapsto(X_0/X_i,\dots,X_n/X_i)$.
    Let $z_{rs}=Z_{rs}/Z_{ij}$, $x_r=X_r/X_i$, and $y_s=Y_s/y_j$, so $k[D_{ij}]\cong k[z_{rs}]_{r,s}$.
    Then
    $$ S^*\colon k[\bA^{(n+1)(m+1)-1}] \to k[\bA^n] \otimes_k k[\bA^m] \cong k[x_0,\dots,x_n,y_0,\dots,y_m] $$
    is given by mapping $z_{rs}=Z_{rs}/Z_{ij}$ to $X_rY_s/X_iY_j=x_ry_s$.
    In particular $S^*(z_{rj})=x_ry_j=x_r$, and similar for $y$, so that $S^*$ is indeed surjective.
    \qed

\eproof

\bdefn

    The closed embedding $S$ in the above proof is called the {\emphcolor Segre embedding}.

\edefn

\bcoro

    The product of projective varieties is projective, and the product of quasi-projective/quasi-affine varieties is quasi-projective/quasi-affine.

\ecoro

\bproof

    For $X\subseteq\bP^n,Y\subseteq\bP^m$ closed, then $X\times Y$ is a closed subvariety of $\bP^n\times\bP^m$, and thus projective.
    The rest follows.
    \qed

\eproof

\subsection{Applications to dimension theory}

\bdefn

    For a variety $X$, consider the morphism $\Delta_X\colon X\to X\times X$ given by $\Delta_X(x)=(x,x)$.
    This is a morphism since its components are the identity; it is called the {\emphcolor diagonal morphism}.

\edefn

Note that the image $\Delta_X(X)=\set{(x,x)}[x\in X]$ is isomorphic to $X$ via $\Delta_X$.
Indeed, its inverse is the composition
$$ \Delta_X(X) \inj X\times X \xtos{\proj_1} X . $$
Thus for every $Y_1,Y_2\subseteq X$, the diagonal morphism forms an isomorphism
$$ Y_1\cap Y_2 \xtos\sim \Delta_X(Y_1\cap Y_2) = \Delta_X(X) \cap (Y_1\times Y_2) . $$
The left-hand side has structure induced from $X$, while the right-hand side's structure is induced from $X\times X$.

\bexam

    Let $X=\bA^n$, then $X\times X=\bA^{2n}$ and $\Delta(\bA^n)\subseteq\bA^{2n}$ is given by $Z(x_1-y_1,\dots,x_n-y_n)$.

\eexam

\bdefn

    A subset $C\subseteq\bA^{n+1}$ is called a {\emphcolor cone} if it is $k^\times$-invariant and $0\in C$.

\edefn

If we let $\pi\colon\bA^{n+1}-0\to\bP^n$ be the projection, we see that $C$ is a cone iff $C=\pi^{-1}(\pi(C-0))\cup0$.
In particular the maps $C\mapsto[C]=\pi(C-0)$ and $X\mapsto C(X)=\pi^{-1}(X)\cup0$ define a bijection between cones $C\subseteq\bA^{n+1}$
and subsets of $\bP^n$.

\blemm

    \benum
        \item For every closed $X\subseteq\bP^n$, its cone $C(X)\subseteq\bA^{n+1}$ is closed.
        \item If $X$ is irreducible, then so is $C(X)$.
        \item $\dim C(X)=\dim X+1$.
    \eenum

\elemm

\bproof

    For $X\subseteq\bP^n$ closed, $X=Z_{\bP^n}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ for homogeneous $f_i$.
    Then $C(X)=Z_{\bA^{n+1}}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ is closed.

    For (2), if $X=Z_{\bP^n}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ is irreducible, then $(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ is prime and thus $C(X)=Z_{\bA^{n+1}}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$
    is irreducible.

    If $X=\bigcup_iX_i$ is a union of closed irreducible subsets, then $C(X)=\bigcup_iC(X_i)$ is a union of closed irreducible subsets.
    Then $\dim C(X)=\max\dim C(X_i)$, so we can assume that $X$ is closed irreducible.
    Since $\pi^{-1}X=C(X)-0$ is open dense in $C(X)$, it is sufficient to show that $\dim\pi^{-1}X=\dim X+1$.

    Consider the standard open affine covering $\bP^n=\bigcup_iU_i$, then $X=\bigcup_iX\cap U_i$ and $\pi^{-1}X=\bigcup\pi^{-1}(X\cap U_i)$.
    So it is sufficient to show
    $$ \dim\pi^{-1}(X\cap U_i) = \dim(X\cap U_i) + 1 . $$
    Notice that $\pi^{-1}U_i=D(x_i)$, and the map
    $$ (\pi,\proj_i)\colon\pi^{-1}U_i \to U_i\times(\bA^1-0) $$
    Is an isomorphism with inverse
    $$ s\colon([x_0:\cdots:x_n],t) \mapsto \parens{\frac{x_0}{x_i}t,\dots,\frac{x_n}{x_i}t} . $$
    These are both clearly inverses, and morphisms.

    Thus
    $$ \pi^{-1}U_i \cong U_i\times(\bA^1-0) , $$
    and this induces an isomorphism
    $$ \pi^{-1}(U_i\cap X) \cong (U_i\cap X)\times(\bA^1-0) . $$
    This has dimension $\dim(U_i\cap X)+1$ as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bthrm

    \benum
        \item Let $X,Y\subseteq\bA^n$ be closed irreducible subvarieties.
        Then every irreducible component of $X\cap Y$ has dimension at least $\dim X+\dim Y-n$.
        In particular if $X\cap Y$ is nonempty, then $\dim(X\cap Y)\geq\dim X+\dim Y-n$.

        \item Let $X,Y\subseteq\bP^n$ be closed irreducible subvarieties such that $\dim X+\dim Y\geq n$.
        Then $X\cap Y$ is nonempty and every irreducible component of $X\cap Y$ is of dimension at least $\dim X+\dim Y-n$.
    \eenum

\ethrm

\bproof

    For (1), there is an isomorphism
    $$ X\cap Y \xtos\sim (X\times Y)\cap\Delta(\bA^n) \subseteq \bA^{2n} . $$
    Since $X$ and $Y$ are irreducible, so is $X\times Y$ and $\dim(X\times Y)=\dim X+\dim Y$.
    Furthermore,
    $$ (X\times Y)\cap\Delta(\bA^n) = Z_{X\times Y}(x_1-y_1,\dots,x_n-y_n) \subseteq X\times Y $$
    is the zeroset of $n$ regular functions and thus the dimension of its irreducible components are each at least
    $\dim(X\times Y)-n=\dim X+\dim Y-n$.

    For (2), we know that $C(X),C(Y)$ are closed irreducible subsets of $\bA^{n+1}$.
    Moreover, $0\in C(X)\cap C(Y)$ so they have nonempty intersection.
    Thus from (1)
    $$ \multline{
        \dim C(X)\cap C(Y) \geq \dim C(X) + \dim C(Y) - (n+1) = (\dim X + 1) + (\dim Y + 1) - (n+1) =\cr
            \dim X + \dim Y - n + 1 \geq 1 .
    } $$
    In particular $C(X)\cap C(Y)$ is not finite, so there is an $x\in C(X)\cap C(Y)-0$ so $\pi(x)\in X\cap Y$, meaning $X\cap Y$
    is nonempty.

    Let $Z\subseteq X\cap Y$ be an irreducible component, then choose $U_i$ such that $Z\cap U_i\neq\emptyset$.
    Then $X\cap U_i,Y\cap U_i$ are closed irreducible subsets of $U_i\cong\bA^n$ and $Z\cap U_i$ is an irreducible component
    of their intersection.
    The result then follows from (1),
    $$ \dim Z = \dim(Z\cap U_i) \geq \dim(X\cap U_i) + \dim(Y\cap U_i) - n = \dim X + \dim Y - n . \qed $$

\eproof

\bcoro

    Let $f_1,\dots,f_m$ be homogeneous non-scalar polynomials in $k[x_0,\dots,x_n]$, with $m\leq n$.
    Then the closed set $Z=Z_{\bP^n}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ is nonempty, and the dimension of each irreducible component of $Z$ is at least $n-m$.

\ecoro

\bproof

    We induct on $m$, for $m=1$ this is clear.
    Let $Z_m=Z_{\bP^n}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ then inductively we have
    $$ Z_{m+1} = Z_m\cap Z_{\bP^n}(f_{m+1}) $$
    and $\dim Z_m+\dim Z_{\bP^n}(f_{m+1})\geq n-m+(n-1)=n+(n-(m+1))\geq n$ since $m+1\leq n$.
    Thus $Z_{m+1}$ is nonempty by the above theorem.
    And an irreducible component of $Z_{m+1}$ has dimension at least $\dim Z_m+\dim Z_{\bP^n}(f_{m+1})-n\geq n-(m+1)$ as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    Let $\phi\colon X\to Y$ be a dominant morphism of affine irreducible varieties.
    Then there exists an open dense subset $U\subseteq Y$ such that $\phi|_{\phi^{-1}U}\colon\phi^{-1}U\to U$ decomposes as
    $$ \phi = \phi^{-1}U \xtos g U\times\bA^n \xtos{\proj_U} U $$
    where $g$ is a finite surjective morphism.

\elemm

\bproof

    Since $\phi$ is dominant, $\phi^*\colon k[Y]\to k[X]$ is injective.
    Since $k[X]$ is a fg $k$-algebra, it is a fg $k[Y]$-algebra.
    Both $X,Y$ are irreducible and thus $k[X],k[Y]$ are domains so we can consider their fraction fields.
    Let $\p=0\subseteq k[Y]$, then the localization $k[X]_\p$ is a fg $k[Y]_\p$-algebra.

    By Noether normalization, there are $y_1,\dots,y_n\in k[X]_\p$ which are algebraically independent over $k(Y)$ and
    $k[X]_\p$ is finite over $k(Y)[y_1,\dots,y_n]$.

    We claim there is an $f\in k[Y]$ such that $y_1,\dots,y_n\in k[X]_f$ and $k[X]_f$ is a finite $k[Y]_f[y_1,\dots,y_n]$-algebra.

    Since $y_1,\dots,y_n\in k[X]_\p$ there is an $f'\in k[Y]$ such that $y_1,\dots,y_n\in k[X]_{f'}$ (take the product of the denominators
    of the $y_i$s).
    Let $a_1,\dots,a_r$ generate $k[X]$ as a $k[Y]$-algebra.
    Then $a_1,\dots,a_r$ are integral over $k(Y)[y_1,\dots,y_n]$ as $k[X]$ is finite over it, and thus integral over $k[Y]_{f''}[y_1,\dots,y_n]$
    for some $f''\in k[Y]$ as we can take common denominators.
    Then $f=f'f''$ does the job.

    With this in mind, the embeddings
    $$ k[Y]_f \inj k[Y]_f[y_1,\dots,y_n] \inj k[X]_f $$
    define morphisms $D_X(f)\xtos gD_Y(f)\times\bA^n\xtos\proj D_Y(f)$ of affine varieties.
    This is because these are all affine and
    $$ k[D_Y(f)\times\bA^n]\cong k[D_Y(f)]\otimes_kk[\bA^n]\cong k[Y]_f\otimes_kk[\bA^n] \cong k[Y]_f[y_1,\dots,y_n] . $$
    Since $k[Y]_f[y_1,\dots,y_n]\inj k[X]_f$ is injective and finite, $g$ is surjective and finite.
    \qed

\eproof

\bthrm

    Let $\phi\colon X\to Y$ be a dominant morphism of irreducible varieties.
    Then $f(X)$ contains a dense open subset $U\subseteq Y$ such that for every $y\in U$, $\phi^{-1}y$ is nonempty and each
    irreducible component of $\phi^{-1}y\subseteq X$ is of dimension $\dim X-\dim Y$.

\ethrm

\bnote

    Previously we showed that for any morphism of irreducible varieties, the fibers have dimension $\geq\dim X-\dim Y$.
    This theorem strengthens this for dominant morphisms.

\enote

\bproof

    For $X$ and $Y$ affine, then by the previous lemma there is a dense open $U\subseteq Y$ such that $\phi$ factors as
    $$ \phi^{-1}U \xtos g U\times\bA^n \xtos\proj U $$
    where $g$ is finite surjective.
    Since $U\subseteq Y$ and $\phi^{-1}U\subseteq X$ are open dense,
    $$ \dim X = \dim\phi^{-1}U = \dim U\times\bA^n = \dim U + n = \dim Y + n . $$
    So $n=\dim X-\dim Y$.

    And for $y\in U$, $g\colon\phi^{-1}U\to U\times\bA^n$ induces a finite surjective morphism $g\colon\phi^{-1}y\to\bA^n$.
    Thus $\dim\phi^{-1}y=n$, so $\phi^{-1}y\neq\emptyset$ and each irreducible component has dimension $\leq n=\dim X-\dim Y$.
    We know in general the dimension is at least $\dim X-\dim Y$ so we have equality.

    In general, we can replace $Y$ with a dense open affine subset $U\subseteq Y$ and $X$ by $\phi^{-1}U$.
    So we can assume that $Y$ is affine.
    Then choose an affine covering $X=\bigcup_iX_i$, then $\phi|_{X_i}\colon X_i\to Y$ is a dominant morphism of affine varieties
    (since $X_i$ is dense, $\phi(X_i)$ is dense in $\phi(X)$ which is then dense in $Y$).
    Thus each $\phi(X_i)$ contains an open dense $U_i$ such that for $y\in U_i$, the irreducible components of
    $\phi|_{X_i}^{-1}y=\phi^{-1}y\cap X_i$ all have dimension $\dim X_i-\dim Y=\dim X-\dim Y$.

    Let $U=\bigcap_iU_i$, then for each $y\in U$, $\phi^{-1}y=\bigcup_i\phi^{-1}y\cap X_i$ is an open cover.
    Since the irreducible components of $\phi^{-1}y\cap X_i$ have dimension $\dim X-\dim Y$, the same is true for $\phi^{-1}y$.
    \qed

\eproof

What kinds of sets can be the images of morphisms of varieties?
If $Y$ is a variety and $X\subseteq Y$ is locally closed, then it itself is a variety and thus the image of the inclusion
$X\inj Y$.
Furthermore, if we have a finite collection of morphisms $\phi_i\colon X_i\to Y$, then they define a morphism
$\phi\colon\coprod_iX_i\to Y$ whose image is the union of images of $\phi_i$.
Thus the possible images of morphisms are closed under finite unions, in particular it contains finite unions of locally
closed sets.

\bdefn

    A {\emphcolor constructible} subset of a topological space is a finite union of locally closed subsets.

\edefn

Note that the collection of constructible subsets of a space $X$ is the minimal collection containing all open subsets
of $X$ that is also closed under finite unions and complements.
This is because it must contain closed sets, as the union of open sets, and be closed under intersections.
Thus it must contain all locally closed sets, as the intersection of closed and open sets, and thus all
constructible sets.

In other words, the collection of constructible subsets of a topological space is the algebra generated by its open subsets.

Chevalley's theorem implies that the image of a morphism of varieties is always constructible.

\bthrm[title={Chevalley}]

    Let $f\colon X\to Y$ be a morphism of varieties.
    Then the image under $f$ of any constructible subset of $X$ is a constructible subset of $Y$.

\ethrm

\bproof

    Since direct images commute with unions, it is sufficient to show that the image of any locally closed subset $S\subseteq X$
    is constructible.
    Since locally closed subsets of a variety are varieties themselves, we can assume that $S=X$ and show that $f(X)$ is
    constructible.

    We claim that for every closed $Z\subseteq X$, $f(Z)\subseteq Y$ is constructible.
    Consider the set of all closed $Z\subseteq X$ such that $f(Z)$ is not constructible.
    If it is non-empty, because $X$ is Noetherian it contains a minimal subset $Z_0$.
    Replacing $X$ by $Z_0$ and $f$ by $f|_{Z_0}$, we show that $f(X)$ is constructible if $f(Z)$ is constructible for all closed
    $Z\subsetneq X$.

    Assume not, then $X$ must be irreducible as otherwise there exists a proper closed union $X=X_1\cup X_2$.
    Then $f(X)=f(X_1)\cup f(X_2)$ is a union of constructible sets and thus constructible.

    Because $X$ is irreducible, so too is $f(X)$ and thus $\cl{f(X)}\subseteq Y$.
    Because being locally closed is transitive, it is sufficient to show that $f(X)$ is constructible in $\cl{f(X)}$.
    So we may assume $\cl{f(X)}=Y$; that is, we may assume $Y$ is irreducible and $f$ is dominant.

    Thus $f(X)$ contains an open dense subset $U\subseteq Y$ such that for every $y\in U$, the fiber $f^{-1}y$ is
    nonempty and all of its irreducible components are of dimension $\dim X-\dim Y$.
    In particular it contains an open subset $U$.
    Since $f(X)=U\cup(f(X)-U)$, it is sufficient to show that $f(X)-U$ is constructible.
    We have that
    $$ f(X) - U = f(X-f^{-1}U) \subseteq Y $$
    and $X-f^{-1}U$ is closed in $X$ and thus by assumption its image is constructible.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    Let $X$ be a Noetherian topological space.
    Then $C\subseteq X$ is closed iff for every irreducible closed subset $Z\subseteq X$ such that $C\cap Z$ is dense in
    $Z$, one has $Z\subseteq C$.

\elemm

\bproof

    If $C$ is closed and $Z\cap C$ is dense in $Z$, then it is closed and dense in $Z$ and thus equal to $Z$.
    So $Z\subseteq C$ as desired.

    Conversely let $\cl C=\bigcup_iZ_i$ be the irreducible components of $\cl C$.
    As $C$ is dense in $\cl C$, $C\cap Z_i$ is dense in $Z_i$ for all $i$.
    Thus $Z_i\subseteq C$ for all $i$ by assumption so $\cl C=C$.
    \qed

\eproof

Now consider the concept of dimension, which is very much a global one.
For example if $X$ is the union of a line and a plane, its dimension is $2$.
But for points lying on the line and not the plane, locally $X$ looks one-dimensional.
This is a bit unfortunate as many properties are local.
So we remedy this.

\bdefn

    Let $X$ be a topological space, and $x\in X$.
    Then $X$'s {\emphcolor Krull dimension at $x$} is
    $$ \dim_xX = \min\set{\dim U}[x\in U\hbox{ open}] . $$

\edefn

Note that if $x\in Z$ is irreducible, then $\dim Z=\dim(Z\cap U)$ for all $U$ open with nonempty intersection with $Z$.
Conversely if $X$ is Noetherian and thus has finitely many irreducible components, $\dim U=\max_Z\dim(Z\cap U)$ where the
maximum runs over the collection of irreducible subsets of $X$.
Thus if $X$ is Noetherian
$$ \dim_xX = \max\set{\dim Z}[x\in Z\hbox{ is an irreducible component of $X$}] . $$
In particular this is true for varieties.

\bthrm

    Let $\phi\colon X\to Y$ be a morphism of varieties.
    Then for all $n$, the set
    $$ \set{x\in X}[\dim_xf^{-1}(f(x))\geq n] \subseteq X $$
    is closed.

\ethrm

\bproof

    We use the previous lemma.
    So let $Z\subseteq X$ be irreducible closed such that $Z^{(n)}=\set{z\in Z}[\dim_xf^{-1}f(z)\geq n]$ is dense in $Z$.
    We want to show that $Z=Z^{(n)}$.

    We can replace $(X,Y)$ by $(f^{-1}(\bar{f(Z)}),\bar{f(Z)})$ and thus assume that $f$ maps $Z$ dominantly into $Y$
    and thus $Y$ is irreducible.
    Let $X_1,\dots,X_m$ be the irreducible components of $X$, and let $f_i\colon X_i\to Y$ be the restriction of $f$ to $X_i$.
    Then define
    $$ Z_i^{(n)} = \set{z\in Z\cap X_i}[\dim_zf_i^{-1}f_i(z)\geq n] $$
    so that $Z^{(n)}=\bigcup_iZ_i^{(n)}$.

    As $Z$ is irreducible and $Z^{(n)}$ is dense in $Z$, it follows that $Z_i^{(n)}$ is dense in $Z$ for some $i$.
    Thus $Z\cap X_i$ is dense in $Z$ for that $i$, and thus $Z\subseteq X_i$.
    So we may replace $X$ with $X_i$ and assume that $X$ is irreducible, and thus $f$ is dominant.

    There is a dense open $U\subseteq Y$ such that for all $y\in U$, $\dim f^{-1}y=\dim X-\dim Y$.
    Because $f$ maps $Z$ dominantly into $Y$, $Z^{(n)}\cap f^{-1}U\neq\emptyset$.
    For $z$ in this intersection
    $$ \dim X - \dim Y = \dim(f^{-1}f(z)) \geq \dim_zf^{-1}f(z) \geq n , $$
    and thus $\dim X-\dim Y\geq n$.

    Conversely, for every $x\in X$ every irreducible component of $f^{-1}f(x)$ has dimension $\geq\dim X-\dim Y\geq n$.
    In particular for $x\in Z$, so $x\in Z^{(n)}$ and thus we have equality as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bnote

    A map $f\colon X\to\bR$ is {\emphcolor upper semicontinuous} if $f^{-1}(-\infty,c)$ is open for all $c\in\bR$.
    Equivalently $f^{-1}[c,\infty)$ is closed.

    Thus this theorem can be reformulated as $x\mapsto\dim_xf^{-1}f(x)$ is upper semicontinuous for any morphism $f$
    of varieties.

\subsection{Separated and complete varieties}

Recall that for a topological space $X$, $X$ is Hausdorff iff its diagonal $\Delta(X)\subseteq X\times X$ is closed.
For varieties, the product $X\times X$ is not the topological product and thus this does not imply Hausdorff-ness.
In fact non-trivial affine, and thus all non-trivial varieties, are not Hausdorff.

Nevertheless this property is still important.

\bdefn

    A variety $X$ is {\emphcolor separated} if its diagonal $\Delta(X)$ is closed in the product variety $X\times X$.

\edefn

For a morphism $\phi\colon X\to Y$ of varieties, let $\Gamma_\phi\colon X\to X\times Y$ be the morphism corresponding to
$(\id,\phi)$, i.e.\ $\Gamma_\phi(x)=(x,\phi(x))$.
Define $\Gamma(\phi)$ to be the image of $\Gamma_\phi$ in $X\times Y$, which then has the induced structure of a SWF.

$\Gamma(\phi)$ is isomorphic to $X$ by the inverse morphisms
$$ X \xtos{\Gamma_\phi} \Gamma(\phi) \inj X\times Y \xtos{\proj_X} X . $$

For example, $\Gamma_\id$ is the diagonal morphism $\Delta_X$ and its image is the diagonal $\Gamma(\id)=\Delta(X)$.

\blemm

    \benum
        \item Let $\phi\colon X\to Y$ be a morphism of varieties with $Y$ separated.
        Then the graph $\Gamma(\phi)\subseteq X\times Y$ is closed.
        \item If $X$ is separated and $Y\subseteq X$ is a subvariety, then $Y$ is separated.
        \item The product of separated varieties is separated.
        \item Quasi-affine and quasi-projective varieties are separated.
    \eenum

\elemm

\bproof

    \benum
        \item Notice that
        $$ \Gamma(\phi) = (\phi\times\id_Y)^{-1}\Delta(Y) \subseteq X\times Y $$
        and it is therefore closed.

        \item Since $X$ is separated, $\Delta(X)\subseteq X\times X$ is closed.
        Thus $\Delta(Y)=\Delta(X)\cap(Y\times Y)\subseteq Y\times Y$ is closed.

        \item Consider the projections $\proj_X\colon(X\times Y)^2\to X^2$ and $\proj_Y\colon(X\times Y)^2\to Y^2$.
        Then
        $$ \Delta(X\times Y) = \proj_X^{-1}(\Delta(X)) \cap \proj_Y^{-1}(\Delta(Y)) $$
        is therefore closed as $X$ and $Y$ are separated.

        \item As quasi-affine and quasi-projective varieties are subvarieties of $\bA^n$ and $\bP^n$, it is sufficient to
        show that these are separated by (2).
        For $\bA^n$ this is clear because $\Delta(\bA^n)=Z(x_1-y_1,\dots,x_n-y_n)\subseteq\bA^n\times\bA^n$.

        Recall the Segre embedding, which is closed,
        $$ S\colon\bP^n\times\bP^n \to \bP^{n^2+2n},\qquad ([x_0:\cdots:x_n],[y_0:\cdots:y_n]) \mapsto [z_{i,j}] , $$
        where $z_{i,j}=x_iy_j$.
        We claim that
        $$ \Delta(\bP^n) = S^{-1}(Z(z_{ij}-z_{ji})) $$
        and this is clearly sufficient.

        Indeed, $S([x_0:\cdots:x_n],[y_0:\cdots:y_n])\in Z(z_{ij}-z_{ji})$ iff $x_iy_j=x_jy_i$ for all $i,j$.
        This is then iff $[x_0:\cdots:x_n]=[y_0:\cdots:y_n]$ as desired.
        \qed
    \eenum

\eproof

\blemm

    For every morphism $\phi\colon X\to Y$, then graph $\Gamma(\phi)\subseteq X\times Y$ is locally closed.

\elemm

\bproof

    Since $\Gamma(\phi)=(\phi\times\id)^{-1}\Delta(Y)$, it is sufficient to show that $\Delta(Y)\subseteq Y\times Y$
    is locally closed.
    Choose an affine covering $Y=\bigcup_iY_i$ and define $W=\bigcup_i(Y_i\times Y_i)$.
    We show that $\Delta(Y)\subseteq W$ is closed.

    Since being closed is a local property, it is sufficient to show that $\Delta(Y)\cap(Y_i\times Y_i)$ is closed.
    Indeed,
    $$ \Delta(Y)\cap(Y_i\times Y_i) = \Delta(Y_i) \subseteq Y_i\times Y_i $$
    is closed since $Y_i$ is affine and thus separated.
    \qed

\eproof

\bprop

    Let $X$ be a variety, then the following are equivalent.
    \benum
        \item $X$ is separated.
        \item For every open affine $U,V\subseteq X$, their intersection is affine, and the natural map
        $k[U]\otimes k[V]\to k[U\cap V]$ is surjective.
        \item There exists an open affine covering $X=\bigcup_iX_i$ such that $X_i\cap X_j$ is affine for all $i,j$
        and $k[X_i]\otimes k[X_j]\to k[X_i\cap X_j]$ is surjective.
    \eenum

\eprop

\bproof

    Assume (1), then since $X$ is separated
    $$ U\cap V \cong \Delta(X)\cap(U\times V) \subseteq U\times V $$
    is closed.
    As $U\times V$ is affine, so too is $U\cap V$.
    Furthermore the restriction map
    $$ k[U]\otimes k[V] \xtos\sim k[U\times V] \to k[U\cap V] $$
    is surjective.
    Notice that the composition
    $$ k[U] \to k[U]\otimes k[V] \xtos\sim k[U\times V] \to k[U\cap V] $$
    is the restriction map, and similarly for $k[V]$.
    Thus the surjection is the natural map as desired, showing (2).

    (2) implies (1) is clear.

    Now suppose that we have an affine covering as in (3).
    Then $X\times X=\bigcup_{i,j}X_i\times X_j$ is an open covering.
    It is sufficient to show that $\Delta(X)\cap(X_i\times X_j)$ is closed in $X_i\times X_j$ for all $i,j$.
    That is, $X_i\cap X_j$ is closed in $X_i\times X_j$.
    Since $X_i\cap X_j$ is affine and $k[X_i\times X_j]\to k[X_i\cap X_j]$ is surjective, we have the desired result.
    \qed

\eproof

\bdefn

    A variety $X$ is {\emphcolor universally closed} if for every other variety $Y$, the projection $X\times Y\to Y$
    is a closed map.

    A separated universally closed variety is called {\emphcolor complete}.

\edefn

\blemm

    \benum
        \item A closed subvariety of a complete variety is complete.
        \item If $\phi\colon X\to Y$ is a morphism of varieties, with $X$ complete and $Y$ separated, then
        $\phi(X)\subseteq Y$ is closed and $\phi(X)$ is a complete variety.
    \eenum

\elemm

\bproof

    \benum
        \item Suppose $Y\subseteq X$ is a closed subvariety of a complete variety.
        Then $Y$ is separated as a subvariety of a separated variety.
        And for every $Z$, projection factorizes as
        $$ Y \times Z \inj X \times Z \to Z $$
        where both maps are closed (the first because $Y$ is closed and the second because $X$ is complete).

        \item $\phi$ factors as
        $$ X \xtos{\Gamma_\phi} X \times Y \xtos{\pi_Y} Y $$
        and so $\phi(X)=\pi_Y(\Gamma(\phi))$.
        Since $Y$ is separated $\Gamma(\phi)$ is closed and since $X$ is complete we see that $\phi(X)$ is closed.

        And $\phi(X)$ is separated because $Y$ is.
        And for any $Z$, consider the surjection $\phi\times\id\colon X\times Z\to\phi(X)\times Z$.
        Then for any closed $C\subseteq\phi(X)\times Z$, the preimage $C'=(\phi\times\id)^{-1}C$ is closed and thus
        $$ \proj^{\phi(X)}_Z(C) = \proj^{\phi(X)}_Z\circ(\phi\times\id)\circ(\phi\times\id)^{-1}(C)
        = \proj^{\phi(X)}_Z\circ(\phi\times\id)(C') = \proj^X_Z(C') $$
        is closed.
        \qed
    \eenum

\eproof

\bexam

    $\bA^1$ is not complete, since the projection $\pi\colon\bA^1\times\bA^1\to\bA^1$ maps
    $$ Z(xy-1) = \set{(x,y)}[xy=1] \mapsto \set{y\neq0} = D(y) \subseteq \bA^1 $$
    which is not closed.

    Alternatively, $\bP^1$ is separated but the image of the embedding $\bA^1\inj\bP^1$ is not closed.
    And thus by the previous lemma, $\bA^1$ is not complete.

\eexam

\bcoro

    If $X$ is complete, then every $f\in k[X]$ is locally constant.

\ecoro

\bproof

    A function $f\in k[X]$ defines a morphism $f\colon X\to\bA^1$ whose image is then closed and complete.
    Thus $f(X)$ is either finite or all of $\bA^1$.
    Since $\bA^1$ is not complete, $f(X)$ is finite.
    Since finite subsets of $\bA^1$ are disconnected, for every connected component $X'$ of $X$, $f(X')$ is
    connected and then must be a point.
    \qed

\eproof

\bcoro

    Quasi-affine complete varieties are finite.

\ecoro

\bproof

    Since $X$ is quasi-affine, every two points in $X$ are separated by a global section.
    That is, for all $a\neq b\in X$ there is an $f\in k[X]$ such that $f(a)\neq f(b)$.
    Indeed, we may assume $X\subseteq\bA^n$ and then one of the coordinate functions does the job.

    Since $X$ is complete, $f$ must be locally constant, and thus $a$ and $b$ must belong to different connected components.
    That is, the singletons of $X$ are the connected components.
    But $X$ has finitely many connected components, as it is Noetherian, so finitely many points.
    \qed

\bnote

    For a complex variety $X$, we can endow it with the {\emphcolor complex analytic topology} as follows.
    For $X=\bA^n$, the underlying set is $\bC^n$, so we equip it with the usual Euclidean topology on $\bC^n$.
    If $X$ is affine, then choose a closed embedding $X\inj\bA^n$ and equip it with the subspace topology.

    Then for a general variety, choose an open affine cover $X=\bigcup_iX_i$, and give it the final topology with
    respect to the inclusions $X_i\inj X$.
    That is, $U\subseteq X$ is open iff $U\cap X_i$ is open for all $i$.
    This is again independent on choice of affine cover.

    Then it can be shown that $X$ is separated/complete iff it is Hausdorff/compact Hausdorff in the complex analytic
    topology.

\subsection{Rational maps}

\blemm

    Let $\phi,\psi\colon X\to Y$ be morphisms of algebraic varieties with $Y$ separated.
    Let $U\subseteq X$ be dense open.
    If $\phi|_U=\psi|_U$ then $\phi=\psi$.

\elemm

\bproof

    Consider $F=(\phi,\psi)\colon X\to Y\times Y$, and since $Y$ is separated $\Delta(Y)\subseteq Y\times Y$ is closed.
    Furthermore $F(U)\subseteq\Delta(Y)$, and so $U\subseteq F^{-1}\Delta(Y)$.
    Since $F^{-1}\Delta(Y)$ is closed, this means $F^{-1}\Delta(Y)=X$, and since
    $$ F^{-1}\Delta(Y) = \set{x\in X}[\phi(x)=\psi(x)] , $$
    we are finished.
    \qed

\eproof

\bnote

    This is not true when $Y$ is not separated.
    For example let $Y=(\bA^1\times\set0)\sqcup(\bA^1\times\set1)/{\sim}$ where we identify $(x,0)\sim(x,1)$ for $x\neq0$.
    Let $\phi,\psi\colon\bA^1\to Y$ be given by $\phi(x)=[(x,0)]$ and $\psi(x)=[(x,1)]$.
    These agree on the open dense $\bA^1-0$ but are not equal at $x=0$.

\enote

Notice that since $\bA^1$ is separated, this means that if $f,g\in k[X]$ agree on a dense open subset, they agree on all of
$X$.
This can also be shown directly, since the set that $f$ and $g$ agree on is $Z(f-g)$ which is closed; if it contains a dense
subset it must be all of $X$.

\bdefn

    Let $X,Y$ be varieties with $Y$ separated.
    A {\emphcolor rational map} is an equivalence class of pairs $(U,\phi_U)$ where $U\subseteq X$ is open dense and
    $\phi_U\colon U\to Y$ is a morphism.
    We identify pairs $(U,\phi_U)\sim(V,\phi_V)$ iff $\phi_U$ and $\phi_V$ agree on an open dense $W\subseteq U\cap V$.
    Note that this is equivalent (by the above lemma) to agreeing on $U\cap V$.

    We write $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ to mean that $\phi$ is a rational map from $X$ to $Y$ (i.e.\ an equivalence class).
    Denote the set of rational maps $X\ratto Y$ by $\rat(X,Y)$.

\edefn

\blemm

    Every rational map $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ has a largest dense open subset $U_\phi\subseteq X$ such that $\phi$
    has a representative $U_\phi\to Y$.

    This $U_\phi$ is called the {\emphcolor domain of definition} of $\phi$.

\elemm

\bproof

    Note that we can glue morphisms together ($\hom(\bul,Y)$ is a sheaf on $X$).
    So let $U_\phi$ be the union of all $U$ where $(U,\phi_U)$ is a representative of $\phi$.
    Since all the $\phi_U$ agree on their intersections this is well-defined and maximal.
    \qed

\eproof

Notice that for every open dense $U\subseteq X$ there is a natural bijection
$$ \rat(X,Y) \cong \rat(U,Y)\qquad\hbox{$U\subseteq X$ open dense} $$
obtained by mapping $(V,\phi_V)\in\rat(X,Y)$ to $(V\cap U,\phi_{V\cap U})$ and $(V,\phi_V)\in\rat(U,Y)$ to
$(V,\phi_V)\in\rat(X,Y)$.

Note that rational maps $X\ratto\bA^1$ are precisely the rational {\it functions\/} we defined to be $k(X)$.
In general, we have that
$$ \rat(X,Y) = \colim_{U\subseteq X}\hom(U,Y) $$
where $U$ range over open dense subsets of $X$.
Thus $\rat(X,Y)$ is immediately seen to be a $k$-algebra.

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be the irreducible components of $X$.
Then for $U\subseteq X$ open dense, $U\cap X_i\subseteq X_i$ is open dense if nonempty.
Thus we have natural maps
$$ \res_{X_i} \colon \rat(X,Y) \to \rat(X_i,Y),\qquad (\phi\colon U\to Y) \mapsto (\phi|_{X_i\cap U}\colon X_i\cap U\to Y) . $$

\blemm

    The product of the natural maps
    $$ \prod_i\res_{X_i} \colon \res(X,Y) \to \prod_i\res(X_i,Y) $$
    is an isomorphism.

\elemm

\bproof

    We construct an explicit inverse.
    For each $i$ consider $X'_i=X_i-\bigcup_{j\neq i}X_j\subseteq X_i$.
    This is disjoint from all other $X_j$ and is open in $X_i$.
    As it is nonempty and $X_i$ is irreducible, it is open dense in $X_i$.
    Thus $X'=\bigsqcup_i X'_i\subseteq X$ is open dense.

    For an $n$-tuple $\phi_i\colon X_i\ratto Y$ with representatives $\phi_{U_i}\colon U_i\to Y$ for $U_i\subseteq X_i$
    open dense, let $U'_i=X_i'\cap U$ and $U=\bigsqcup_iU'_i$.
    Then $U\subseteq X'\subseteq X$ is open dense, and since the $U'_i$ are disjoint there is a unique $\phi\colon U\to Y$
    such that $\phi|_{U'_i}=\phi_i|_{U'_i}$ for all $i$.

    Mapping $(\phi_i)_i\mapsto\phi$ is the desired inverse.
    \qed

\eproof

In lieu of this lemma, we may assume that $X$ is irreducible.

\bdefn

    A rational map $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ is {\emphcolor dominant} if $\phi_U\colon U\to Y$ is dominant for some representative
    of $\phi$.
    This implies every representative of $\phi$ is dominant.

    If $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ and $\psi\colon Y\ratto Z$ are rational with $\phi$ dominant, we form their composition
    $\psi\circ\phi\colon X\ratto Z$ as follows.
    For any representatives $\phi_U\colon U\to Y,\psi_V\colon V\to Z$, let $W=\phi_U^{-1}V$ which is nonempty since $\phi_U$
    is dominant.
    Then define $\psi\circ\phi$ to be the class of $\psi_V\circ(\phi_U|_W)$.
    This is independent on choice of representatives.

\edefn

We can thus form a category whose objects are separated varieties over $k$ and whose morphisms are the
dominant rational maps between them.
We will primarily focus on the subcategory whose objects are furthermore irreducible.
Call these categories $\RatVar_k$ and $\RatVar^\irr_k$ respectively.
We let their hom-sets be denoted by $\rat_\dom(X,Y)$.

Furthermore, a dominant rational map $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ defines the pullback $\phi^*\colon k(Y)\to k(X)$
by $\phi^*(f)=f\circ\phi$.
We can compose these maps and we see that $(\phi_1\circ\phi_2)^*=\phi_2^*\circ\phi_1^*$.
This defines a contravariant functor from the aforementioned category to the category of $k$-algebras
As we consider the subcategory consisting of irreducible separated varieties, this functor maps into field extensions of $k$.

Recall that if $X$ is affine (and irreducible) then $k(X)$ is the field of fractions of $k[X]$, and then
$\phi^*\colon k(X)\to k(Y)$ is the unique extension of $\phi^*\colon k[X]\to k[Y]$.

\bdefn

    Let $X,Y$ be irreducible separable varieties.
    A {\emphcolor birational map} is a dominant rational map $X\ratto Y$ which is an isomorphism in the category
    $\RatVar^\irr$.

    $X$ and $Y$ are {\emphcolor birationally equivalent} if they are isomorphic in $\RatVar^\irr$.

\edefn

Since there is a natural embedding $\hom_\Var(X,Y)\inj\rat(X,Y)$, we can also call a dominant morphism of
(separable, irreducible) varieties a birational map if the associated rational map is.

\bexam

    If $U\subseteq X$ is open dense, then the inclusion $\iota\colon U\inj X$ is a birational equivalence.
    Its inverse is the rational map $X\ratto U$ represented by $(U,\id_U)$.

\eexam

Note that for every birational equivalence $\alpha\colon X'\ratto X$, the induced map
$$ \alpha^*\colon\rat(X,Y) \to \rat(X',Y),\qquad \phi\mapsto\phi\circ\alpha $$
is a bijection.
Its inverse is given by precomposition with $\alpha$'s inverse.

Postcomposition is only defined for dominant rational maps, in which case it is still a bijection.
That is, for $\alpha\colon Y\ratto Y'$ postcomposition is a bijection:
$$ \alpha_*\colon\rat_\dom(X,Y) \xtos\sim \rat_\dom(X,Y'),\qquad \phi\mapsto\alpha\circ\phi . $$

\bthrm

    There is an antiequivalence of categories $\RatVar^\irr_k$ and finite field extensions of $k$.
    The equivalence is given by mapping $X$ to $k(X)$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi^*$.

\ethrm

\bproof

    We need to show that the functor is fully faithful and essentially surjective.
    We reduce full faithfullness to the fact that global sections is fully faithful on affine varieties.

    Because $X,Y$ are birationally equivalent to their nonempty open subsets, we can replace them with any affine subset.
    So we may assume $X,Y$ are affine.
    We first show that $\phi\mapsto\phi^*$ is injective.
    If $\phi_1,\phi_2\colon X\ratto Y$ are rational maps, we can replace $X$ with an affine subset of the intersection of the
    domains of $\phi_1,\phi_2$.
    So $\phi_1^*,\phi_2^*\colon k(Y)\to k(X)$ are equal, and thus their restrictions $\phi_1^*,\phi_2^*\colon k[Y]\to k[X]$
    are equal.
    Since global sections is fully faithful on affine varieties, $\phi_1=\phi_2$.

    Now let $\psi\colon k(Y)\to k(X)$ be a $k$-algebra morphism.
    Since $k[Y]\subseteq k(Y)$ is a finitely generated $k$-subalgebra, there is a $f\in k[X]$ such that
    $\psi(k[Y])\subseteq k[X]_f=k[D_X(f)]$.
    Thus $\psi|_{k[Y]}$ is a morphism $k[Y]\to k[D_X(f)]$ and thus comes from a morphism $\phi\colon D_X(f)\to Y$ of
    affine varieties.
    Then $\phi$ defines a rational map $D_X(f)\ratto Y$ whose pullback is $\psi$.

    For essential surjectivity, let $L/k$ be a finite field extension.
    Suppose $a_1,\dots,a_n\in L$ generate the field extension.
    Let $A=k[a_1,\dots,a_n]$ so that $L=\Frac(A)$ and $A$ is a domain, finitely generated over $k$.
    Thus there is an affine variety $X$ such that $k[X]\cong A$ and thus
    $$ k(X) \cong \Frac(k[X]) \cong \Frac(A) \cong L $$
    as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

The following two corollaries are immediate.

\bcoro

    Let $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ be a dominant rational map between irreducible separated varieties.
    The following are equivalent.
    \benum
        \item $\phi$ is birational.
        \item There exist dense open subsets $U\subseteq X,V\subseteq Y$ such that $\phi$ induces an isomorphism
        $U\xtos\sim V$.
        \item The pullback $\phi^*\colon k(Y)\to k(X)$ is an isomorphism.
    \eenum

\ecoro

\bcoro

    Let $X,Y$ be two irreducible separated varieties.
    The following are equivalent.
    \benum
        \item $X$ and $Y$ are birationally equivalent.
        \item There exist dense open subsets $U\subseteq X,V\subseteq Y$ such that $U$ and $V$ are isomorphic varieties.
        \item $k(X)\cong k(Y)$.
    \eenum

\ecoro

\subsection{Curves}

\bdefn

    Let $X$ be a variety and $x\in X$.
    The {\emphcolor cotangent space} of $X$ at $x$ is the $k$-vector space $T_x^*X=\m_x/\m_x^2$ where $\m_x$ is the maximal
    ideal of the stalk $\O_{X,x}$.
    The {\emphcolor tangent space} of $X$ at $x$ is the dual space $T_xX=(\m_x/\m_x^2)^*$.

\edefn

The motivation for this definition is due to the following definition and result.

\bdefn

    Assume $X=Z_{\bA^n}(f_1,\dots,f_m)$ is an affine variety, and let $x\in X$.
    The {\emphcolor Jacobian} of $X$ at $x$ is the linear map $J_X(x)\colon k^m\to k^n$ given by
    $$ \pmatrix{
        \pdvof{f_1}{x_1}(x)&\cdots&\pdvof{f_k}{x_1}(x)\cr
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots\cr
        \pdvof{f_1}{x_k}(x)&\cdots&\pdvof{f_k}{x_k}(x)
    } . $$

\edefn

\bprop

    Let $x\in X$ be a point in an algebraic variety.
    Then $T_x^*X\cong\coker(J_X(x))$.

\eprop

\bdefn

    A point $x\in X$ is a {\emphcolor regular} (or {\emphcolor nonsingular/smooth}) point of $X$ if $\dim_k(T_x^*X)=\dim_xX$.
    A variety is {\emphcolor regular} (or {\emphcolor nonsingular/smooth}) if all of its points are so.

\edefn

The following two corollaries are then immediate.

\bcoro

    A point $x\in X$ is regular iff $\corank(J_X(x))=n-\rank(J_X(x))=\dim_xX$.

\ecoro

\bcoro

    Let $X=Z_{\bA^n}(f)$, with $f$ nonconstant.
    Then by Krull's principal ideal theorem $\dim_xX=n-1$.
    The Jacobian of $X$ at $x$ is just the gradient $\nabla f(x)$, and $X$ is smooth at $x$ iff that gradient is nonzero.

\ecoro

\bdefn

    A variety $X$ is {\emphcolor equidimensional} if all of its irreducible components have the same dimension.
    Equivalently $\dim_xX$ is constant for all $x\in X$.

\edefn

\bdefn

    A {\emphcolor curve} is an equidimensional variety of dimension $1$.

\edefn

Now we make a few remarks.
If $A$ is a Noetherian ring, and $S\subseteq A$ is a multiplicative subset, then ideals of $S^{-1}A$ are of the form $S^{-1}I$.
Thus $S^{-1}A$ is Noetherian: if we have a collection of ideals $\Sigma$ of $S^{-1}A$ let $\Sigma_A$ be the collection of ideals $I\leq A$
such that $S^{-1}I\in\Sigma$.
Then $\Sigma_A$ has a maximal ideal whose localization is then a maximal ideal of $\Sigma$.

In particular if $Y$ is a variety, then $\O_{Y,y}$ is Noetherian.
Indeed, if $y\in U$ is an affine neighborhood then $\O_{Y,y}$ is isomorphic to a localization of $k[U]$ which is Noetherian (by Hilbert's basis theorem).

Furthermore, for any $y\in Y$, $\dim_yY=\dim(\O_{Y,y})$ where the right-hand side is the Krull dimension of the ring, i.e.\ the supremum on the length of chains of prime ideals.
Indeed, because this claim is local we can assume $Y$ is affine.
Then $\O_{Y,y}=k[Y]_{\m_y}$, so prime ideals of the stalk are in one-to-one correspondence with prime ideals of $k[Y]$ contained in $\m_y$.
These correspond, in an order-reversing manner, to closed irreducible subsets of $Y$ via $Z(\bul)$ which contain $Z(\m_y)=\set y$.
So any chain of prime ideals of $k[Y]_{\m_y}$ gives rise to a chain of closed irreducible subsets of $Y$ containing $y$, which gives the desired result.

Also if $Y$ is irreducible, then $\O_{Y,y}$ can be viewed as a subring of $k(Y)$.
This is obvious considering their definitions via colimits or even via germs.
We can then view $k(Y)$ as $\Frac(\O_{Y,y})$.

Thus if $X$ is a curve, so $\dim_xX=1$ for all $x\in X$ and thus its stalks $\O_{X,x}$ are all Noetherian rings of dimension $1$.
Furthermore if $X$ is smooth at $x\in X$ then $1=\dim_xX=\dim_k\m_x/\m_x^2$.
If $\pi\in\m_x$ generates the $1$-dimensional space $\m_x/\m_x^2$, by Nakayama $\pi$ generates the ideal $\m_x$, so it is principal.
This leads to the following definition.

\bdefn

    A {\emphcolor discrete valuation ring} ({\emphcolor DVR}) is a one-dimensional Noetherian local ring $(A,\m)$ for which $\m$ is principal.
    A generator of $\m$ is called a {\emphcolor uniformizer} of $A$.

\edefn

So stalks of curves at smooth points are DVRs.

By Nakayama, $\pi\in\m$ is a uniformizer iff $\bar\pi\in\m/\m^2$ generates the module over $A/\m\cong k$.
Because $\m\neq\m^2$, as otherwise $0\in\m$ would generate it by Nakayama and thus $\m=0$, we see that $\pi\in\m$ is a uniformizer iff $\pi\in\m-\m^2$.
This is because such a uniformizer must exist, so $\dim_k\m/\m^2=1$ and so any non-zero element generates it.

\blemm

    Let $(A,\m)$ be a DVR and $\pi\in A$ be a uniformizer.
    Then $A$ is a domain and every nonzero element of $A$ can be written as $\pi^nu$ for a unique $n\in\bZ_{\geq0}$ and $u\in A^\times$.

\elemm

\bproof

    Let $0\neq a\in A$.
    If $a$ is not a unit, then $a\in\m$ so $a=\pi a_1$ for some $0\neq a_1\in A$.
    If $a_1$ is not a unit, we can continue this.
    Thus we get an increasing chain of ideals $(a)\subseteq(a_1)\subseteq\cdots$.
    Note that this chain is strictly increasing: if $a_{m+1}\in(a_m)$ then $a_{m+1}=a_mb$ for $b\in A$, so $a_m=\pi a_{m+1}=\pi ba_m$.
    Thus $(1-\pi b)a_m=0$, but $1-\pi b\notin\m$ and is thus invertible so $a_m=0$, and thus $a=0$ a contradiction.

    So this construction must terminate; at some point $a_n=u$ is a unit.
    And $a=\pi^nu$ as desired.

    To show that $A$ is a domain, because its elements can be written as $\pi^nu$, it is sufficient to show that $\pi$ is not nilpotent.
    If it were, then $\m^r=0$ for some $r>0$.
    Thus $\m\subseteq\nil(A)$ and so $\dim A=\dim(A/\nil(A))=\dim(A/\m)=0$ since $A/\m$ is a field.
    But by assumption $A$ has dimension one, so it is a domain.

    Now for uniqueness of representation, it is sufficient to show that $\pi^n$ cannot be written as $\pi^mu$ for $n\neq m,u\in A^\times$.
    So suppose $\pi^n=\pi^mu$, and we can assume $n>m$.
    Then $\pi^m(u-\pi^{n-m})=0$ so because $A$ is a domain, $u=\pi^{n-m}$ which means $\pi^{n-m}$, and thus $\pi$, is a unit.
    But this is impossible.
    \qed

\eproof

\bprop

    Let $\phi\colon X\ratto Y$ be a rational map from a curve $X$ to a projective (and thus separated) variety $Y$.
    Then every smooth point of $X$ lies in the domain of definition of $\phi$.

\eprop

\bproof

    First assume that $Y=\bP^n$, and let $x\in X$ be a smooth point.
    Then $\O_{X,x}$ is a DVR and let $\pi\in\O_{X,x}$ be a uniformizer.
    Thus $\pi\in\O_X(U)$ for some open $x\in U$.
    Then $Z_U(\pi)$ is a finite set containing $x$, and by potentially shrinking $U$ we may assume $Z_U(\pi)=\set x$.
    We can shrink $U$ further still so that $\phi$ defines a morphism $U-x\to Y$ (since $\phi$'s domain of definition is dense, it intersects $U$).
    By restricting $\phi$ to the primage of some $V_i=D_{\bP^n}(X_i)\cong\bA^n$, we may assume that $\phi$ lands in $\bA^n$ (as the $V_i$ cover $\bP^n$, one of the preimages must intersect $U$).

    Thus
    $$ \phi|_{U-x} = \pi\circ(f_0,\dots,f_i=1,\dots,f_n) $$
    for $f_j\in\O_X(U-x)$ and $\pi\colon\bA^{n+1}-0\to\bP^n$ is the projection.
    Then each $f_j$ lies in $k(X)=\Frac(\O_{X,x})$, and so $f_j$ can be written uniquely as $f_j=\pi^{m_j}u_j$ for $m_j\in\bZ$ and $u_j\in\O_{X,x}^\times$ (note here because it is in the fraction field,
    $m_j$ may be nonnegative).
    Thus, by potentially shrinking $U$ further, each $u_j$ defines a non-vanishing regular function.

    Let $m=\min_jm_j$ so that $\pi^{-m}f_j$ is regular on $U$, since $\pi^{-m}f_j$ is a nonnegative power of $\pi$ times $u_j$.
    Thus $\pi\circ(\pi^{-m}f_0,\dots,\pi^{-m}f_n)$ is a regular function on all of $U$.
    Furthermore it agrees with $\phi$ on $U-x$ (since $\pi^{-m}f_i$ is a nonzero multiple of $f_i$ and thus projecting it gives the same value as $f_i$).
    Thus we can extend $\phi$ to include $x$ in its domain, as desired.

    For an arbitrary projective variety $Y$, it is closed in some $\bP^n$.
    Let $\iota\colon Y\inj\bP^n$ be the inclusion, so as before $\iota\circ\phi$ extends over some open neighborhood $V$ of $x$.
    Let us denote this extended morphism by $\psi$, so $\psi^{-1}Y\subseteq V$.
    Thus $\psi^{-1}Y$ is closed in $V$ and also contains a dense open subset (the subset on which $\phi$ is defined) and thus must be equal to $V$.
    So $\psi$ is a morphism $V\to Y$, so $\phi$ can be extended to a neighborhood of $x$, $V$.
    \qed

\eproof

\bnote

    Both assumptions in the above result, that $Y$ is projective and that $x\in X$ is smooth, are critical.
    For example, the rational map $\bP^1\ratto\bA^1$ defined to be the the identity on a copy of $\bA^1\subseteq\bP^1$ does not extend to $\infty\in\bP^1$ (which is the point whose first/second coordinate is zero).
    This is because it would define a non-constant morphism $\bP^1\to\bA^1$, which projective spaces do not possess ($k[\bP^n]=k$).

    And we need smoothness, as if we consider $X=Z_{\bA^2}(y^2-x^2-x^3)$ and the map $X-0\to\bP^1$ given by $(x,y)\mapsto[y:x]$, we cannot extend this $(0,0)$.

\enote

Recall our antiequivalence of categories
$$ \RatVar^\irr_k\cong k/{\sf Field}^{\sf fin},\qquad X\mapsto k(X),\ \phi\mapsto\phi^* . $$
Note here that $k/{\sf Field}^{\sf fin}$ is the category of finite field extensions of $k$.
This is denoted by $k/{\sf Field}$ because it corresponds to a general categorical construction, the undercategory, whose objects are morphisms $k\to L$.
This conflicts with the notation $L/k$ for a field extension, which is unfortunate.
In any case, we will not use this notation again.

We claim that this equivalence of categories restricts to an equivalence of categories
$$ \set{\hbox{irreducible smooth projective curves}} \cong \set{\hbox{finite field extensions $L/k$ of transcendence degree $1$}} . $$
Firstly, if $X$ is an irreducible smooth projective curve, then we need to show that $k(X)/k$ has transcendence degree $1$.
If we let $x\in X$ be arbitrary, then $k(X)=\Frac(\O_{X,x})$ and $\O_{X,x}$ is a DVR and thus has a uniformizer $\pi\in\O_{X,x}$.
Elements of $k(X)$ are of the form $\pi^mu$ for $u\in\O_{X,x}^\times$ and $m\in\bZ$, and so $\pi$ generates the extension.
Since $\dim\O_{X,x}=1$, $k(X)$ is a proper extension of $k$ and thus $\pi$ is a transcendence basis.

Thus the functor $X\mapsto k(X),\phi\mapsto\phi^*$ is well-defined and remains fully faithful.

To show essential surjectivity we must work a little harder.
The idea is that given $L$, there is an irreducible affine variety $X$ with $k(X)\cong L$ (as before).
Furthermore it is a curve since $\dim X=\dim_kL=1$.
It is then closed in some $\bA^n$ which we can embed in $\bP^n$ in the usual manner.
Taking the Zariski closure of $X\subseteq\bP^n$, we obtain an irreducible projective curve $\bar X$ with the same function field $L$.
The issue is that the curve may not be smooth, which we remedy by ``smoothening it out''.

\bdefn

    A variety $X$ is {\emphcolor normal} when the local ring $\O_{X,x}$ is a normal integral domain for all $x\in X$.
    Recall that an integral domain being normal means being integrally closed in its field of fractions.

\edefn

\blemm

    Suppose $x\in X$ lies in more than one irreducible component of $X$.
    Then $\O_{X,x}$ is not a domain; thus normal varieties are disjoint unions of irreducible varieties.

\elemm

\bproof

    Recall that the minimal prime ideals of $\O_{X,x}$ are in bijection with the irreducible components of $X$ containing $x$.
    A domain has a single minimal prime ideal: the zero ideal.
    Thus if $x$ lies in more than a single irreducible component, $\O_{X,x}$ cannot be a domain.
    \qed

\eproof

If $X$ is normal, then by this lemma $X$ is the disjoint union of its irreducible components: $X=\bigsqcup_iX_i$.
Thus each irreducible component is open and closed.
Since being normal at a point is clearly local, this means that all the $X_i$ are normal subvarieties as well.

\bdefn

    For a variety $X$, its {\emphcolor normalization} is a normal variety $\bar X$ along with a dominant morphism $\nu\colon\bar X\to X$ which is universal.
    That is, for any other dominant morphism from a normal variety $\phi\colon Y\to X$, there is a unique $\psi\colon Y\to\bar X$ such that $\phi=\nu\circ\psi$.

\edefn

As this is a universal property, normalization is unique up to unique isomorphism.
We will show that every variety has a normalization.

\blemm

    \benum
        \item If $\nu\colon\bar X\to X$ is dominant from a normal variety, it is a normalization iff for every {\it irreducible\/} and normal $Y$ and dominant $\phi\colon Y\to X$,
        there is a unique $\psi\colon Y\to\bar X$ such that $\phi=\nu\circ\psi$.

        \item Let $X$ be a variety with irreducible components $X_1,\dots,X_n$.
        Suppose that the normalization $\bar X_i$ exists for each $i$.
        Then $\bar X=\coprod_i\bar X_i$ is the normalization of $X$.
    \eenum

    Thus we can restrict the construction of normalizations to irreducible varieties.

\elemm

\bproof

    For (1), one direction is clear.
    In the other, suppose $\phi\colon Y\to X$ is dominant and $Y$ is normal.
    Then as $Y$ is normal, it is the disjoint union of its irreducible components, $Y=\bigsqcup_iY_i$ which are all clopen subsets and thus normal.
    By assumption, we have unique morphisms $\psi_i\colon Y_i\to\bar X$ such that $\phi|_{Y_i}=\nu\circ\psi_i$.
    Then letting $\psi=\bigsqcup_i\psi_i\colon Y\to\bar X$ we see that $\phi=\nu\circ\psi$, and uniqueness of $\psi$ follows from the uniqueness of the $\psi_i$s.

    For (2) let $\nu_i\colon\bar X_i\to X_i$ be the normalization maps so $\nu=\bigsqcup_i\nu_i\colon\bar X\to X$.
    As being normal is local, $\bar X$ is normal, and since $\nu(\bar X)\cap X_i$ is dense in $X_i$ for each $i$ and $X_i\subseteq X$ is closed, $\nu(\bar X)$ is dense in $X$.
    So $\nu$ is dominant.

    Now suppose $\phi\colon Y\to X$ is dominant from irreducible normal $Y$.
    Then $\phi(Y)\subseteq X$ is irreducible and thus contained in some $X_i$.
    Thus there is some $\psi\colon Y\to\bar X_i\subseteq\bar X$ such that $\phi=\nu_i\circ\psi$; thus $\phi=\nu\circ\psi$ as desired.
    By (1) this means that $\nu\colon\bar X\to X$ is the normalization.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    Let $A$ be an integral domain.
    Then $A$ is normal iff $A_\m$ is normal for all maximal $\m\leq A$.

\elemm

\bproof

    Let $B$ be the integral closure of $A$ in $\Frac(A)$.
    Since forming the intgeral closure commutes with localization, $B_\m$ is the integral closure of $A_\m$ in $\Frac(A)_\m=\Frac(A)$.
    Thus $A_\m$ is normal for every $\m$ iff $0=B_\m/A_\m=(B/A)_\m$ for all $\m$.
    This holds iff $B/A=0$, i.e.\ iff $A$ is normal.
    \qed

\eproof

Thus if $A$ is a fg $k$-algebra which is a domain, then $A$ is normal then iff $X=\specm(A)$ is.
Indeed, we know that $\O_{X,x}$ is the localization of $k[X]=A$ by the maximal ideal corresponding to $x\in\specm(A)$.
By the above lemma, $A$ is normal iff all of these are normal, i.e.\ iff $X$ is normal.

We now state the following result without proof.

\bthrm

    Let $k$ be a field and $A$ a fg $k$-algebra that is a domain.
    Then $\bar A$, the integral closure of $A$ in its fraction field $L$, is a finite $A$-algebra.

\ethrm

\bnote

    For a proof, see {\it Matsumura, Commutative Algebra\/} theorem 72 part 31.

\enote

Thus if we have an affine variety $X=\specm(A)$ with $A$ an fg domain over $k$, then we also obtain an affine variety $\specm(\bar A)$.

\bprop

    Let $A$ ba a fg $k$-algebra that is a domain, and let $X=\specm(A)$ be its corresponding irreducible affine variety.
    Let $\bar A$ be $A$'s integral closure in its fraction field, and let $\bar X=X$.
    Then the natural map $\nu\colon\bar X\to X$ is the normalization of $X$.

\eprop

\bproof

    First suppose that $Y=\specm(B)$ is an irreducible normal affine variety, and we have a dominant morphism $\phi\colon Y\to X$.
    This corresponds to an injective $k$-algebra morphism $\phi^*\colon A\inj B$.
    This extends to a morphism of their field of fractions, which we denote by $\phi^*$ as well.
    Then $\phi^*(\bar A)$ is contained inside the integral closure of $B$ in $\Frac(B)$, which is just $B$ as $Y$, and thus $B$, is normal.
    So $\phi^*$ extends to a map $\phi^*\colon\bar A\to B$ through which $\nu$ factors.

    This proves the universal property in the affine case.

    Now suppose that $Y$ is an arbitrary irreducible normal variety and there is a dominant map $\phi\colon Y\to X$.
    Let $U_i$ be an affine open cover of $Y$.
    Then each $U_i$ is irreducible, normal, and affine, and $\phi|_{U_i}\colon U_i\to X$ is dominant.
    Thus there are unique $\psi_i\colon U_i\to\bar X$ such that $\phi|_{U_i}=\nu\circ\psi_i$.

    These $\psi_i$ must agree on the intersections $U_i\cap U_j$, which can be seen by considering an affine cover of $U_i\cap U_j$.
    Thus we glue $\psi_i$ together to get $\psi\colon Y\to\bar X$ which restricts to $\psi_i$ on each $U_i$.
    Thus $\phi=\nu\circ\psi$, and uniqueness of $\psi$ is due to $\psi_i$'s uniqueness.
    \qed

\eproof

\bprop

    The normalization $\nu\colon\bar X\to X$ exists for every variety $X$, and is a finite surjective birational morphism.

\eprop

\bproof

    We may assume that $X$ is irreducible.
    If we take an open affine cover $U_i$ of $X$, these are all irreducible affine varieties and thus possess a normalization $\bar U_i\to U_i$.
    By universality, we can uniquely identify $\bar U_i$ and $\bar U_j$ over $U_i\cap U_j$.
    Then by gluing the $\bar U_i$ together, we get the desired normalization.

    To show that the normalization is finite surjective birational, we may work locally on $X$ and thus assume that $X$ is irreducible affine.
    So let $X=\specm(A)$ and then $\bar X=\specm(\bar A)$, where $A$ is a fg $k$-algebra which is a domain and $\bar A$ is its integral closure in $\Frac(A)$.
    Because $\bar A$ is also a domain, $\bar X$ is irreducible as well.

    So
    $$ k(X) \cong \Frac(A) \cong \Frac(\bar A) \cong k(\bar X) , $$
    which means $X$ and $\bar X$ are birationally equivalent.
    And $\bar A$ is a finite $A$-algebra, so $\nu$ is finite.
    Because finite morphisms are closed, and $\nu$ is dominant, $\nu$ is surjective.
    \qed

\eproof

We return now to curves; the relevance of normalization is due to the following result.

\bprop

    A $1$-dimensional Noetherian local domain $(A,\m)$ is a DVR iff $A$ is normal.

\eprop

\bproof

    Because every element of a DVR is of the form $\pi^nu$ for unique $n\in\bZ_{\geq0}$ and $u\in A^\times$, it follows immediately that DVRs are UFDs.
    UFDs are normal (the proof is the same as the case of $\bZ$), so one direction is clear.

    Conversely if $A$ is normal, we will show that $\m$ is principal.
    Notice that since $A$ is $1$-dimensional, the only prime ideals are $0\subsetneq\m$.
    Furthermore, $\m\neq\m^2$ as otherwise by Nakayama we would have that $\m=0$ so that $A$ is a field and then not one-dimensional.
    So let $x\in\m-\m^2$, and let $0\neq y\in\m$.

    Then $\m$ is no longer a proper ideal in the localization $A_y$, and thus $A_y$ is a zero-dimensional domain (as its only prime ideal is $0$).
    Thus it is a field, so $1/x\in A_y$.
    Thus $y^m\in(x)$ for some $m>0$.
    Since $\m$ is fg and $y\in\m$ was arbitrary, $\m^n\subseteq(x)$ for some $n>0$ which we choose to be minimal; we claim $n=1$ so that $\m=(x)$.

    Now let $z\in\m^{n-1}-(x)$, and then $z\m\subseteq\m^n\subseteq(x)$.
    Define $\alpha=z/x$ so $\alpha\in K=\Frac(A)$.
    But $\alpha\notin A$, and $\alpha\m\subseteq A$ so it is an ideal of $A$.

    If $\alpha\m\leq\m$ then $\m$ is a faithful fg $A[\alpha]$-module over $A$.
    This would then imply that $\alpha$ is integral over $A$.
    But $A$ is normal, which would mean $\alpha\in A$, a contradiction.

    Thus $\alpha\m$ is not contained in $\m$ and thus it is not a proper ideal, $\alpha\m=A$.
    In particular $x/z\in\m$ and so $z\m=(x)$.
    But then $x\in z\m\subseteq\m^n$, and so if $n\geq2$ this would mean $x\in\m^2$ a contradiction.
    Thus $n=1$ as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\bcoro

    A curve $X$ is smooth at $x\in X$ iff it is normal at $\O_{X,x}$.
    Thus an irreducible curve $X$ is smooth iff it is normal.

\ecoro

We may now complete the proof of essential surjectivity of our functor $X\mapsto k(X)$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi^*$.
So let $L/k$ be a finite field extension of transcendence degree $1$, which determines an irreducible affine variety $X$ such that $k(X)\cong L$.
There is then a closed embedding $X\subseteq\bA^r$, which we can then embed as open dense in $\bP^r$.
Let $\cl X\subseteq\bP^r$ denote the Zariski closure, which is then an irreducible projective variety with a dense open subvariety $X$, so $k(\bar X)\cong k(X)$.
Let $\nu\colon Y\to\bar X$ be its normalization, so $Y$ is an irreducible normal curve and thus a smooth curve by the above corollary.
Furthermore since $\nu$ is finite surjective,
$$ k(Y) \cong k(\bar X) \cong k(X) \cong L . $$
It remains to show that $Y$ is projective.

\blemm

    Let $\phi\colon X\to Y$ be a finite morphism such that $Y$ is complete.
    Then $X$ is also complete.

\elemm

\bproof

    Universal closure is clear, since for a variety $Z$, projection $\proj^X_Z\colon X\times Z\to Z$ factors as
    $$ X \times Z \xtos{\phi\times\id} Y \times Z \xtos{\proj^Y_Z} Z $$
    which is a composition of closed maps.

    We now need to show that $X$ is separated.
    Now since $\phi$ is finite, it is closed and thus so is $\phi\times\phi$.
    So $\phi(X\times X)\subseteq Y\times Y$ and $\phi(\Delta X)\subseteq\Delta Y$ are closed.
    Thus $\phi(\Delta X)\subseteq\phi(X\times X)$ is closed, and thus taking preimages $X\times_YX=\set{(x_0,x_1)\in X\times X}[\phi x_0=\phi x_1]\subseteq X\times X$ is closed.

    Now we claim that $\Delta X\subseteq X\times_YX$ is closed, and this is sufficient.
    Because $\phi$ is affine, we may assume that $X$ and $Y$ are.
    Indeed, let $U_i$ be an affine cover of $Y$, then $V_i=\phi^{-1}U_i$ is an affine cover of $X$.
    Then
    $$ \Delta X = \bigcup_i\Delta(V_i),\qquad X\times_YX = \bigcup_i V_i\times_{U_i}V_i , $$
    and so if $\Delta(V_i)\subseteq V_i\times_{U_i}V_i$ is closed for all $i$, so is $\Delta X\subseteq X\times_YX$.

    But affine varieties are separated, so $\Delta(X)\subseteq X^2$ is closed.
    Thus $\Delta(X)\subseteq X\times_YX$ is closed as well, as desired.
    \qed

\eproof

\blemm

    Let $X$ be projective and $x_1,\dots,x_n\in X$.
    Then there is an affine open neighborhood of $X$ containing all of the $x_i$.

\elemm

\bproof

    If $X=\bP^m$, we note that $\gl_{m+1}(k)$ acts continuously and transitively on $\bP^m$.
    Thus every matrix in $\gl_{m+1}(k)$ forms an automorphism of $\bP^m$.
    We claim that there is a $g\in\gl_{m+1}(k)$ such that the first rows of $g(x_1),\dots,g(x_n)$ are all non-zero and thus lie in $D_{\bP^m}(X_1)$.
    We construct such a $g$ as follows.

    First, there is a linear functional $\ell\in(k^{n+1})^*$ such that $\ell(x_i)\neq0$ for all $i$.
    Indeed, define the subspace $H_i=\set{\ell\in(k^{n+1})^*}[\ell(x_i)=0]$; it is proper since $x_i\neq0$.
    Then $\bigcup_iH_i$ is not the whole dual space, since the union of subspaces is only a subspace if they are all contained in one of the subspaces.
    Thus there is an $\ell$ not in any $H_i$, as desired.
    Now extend $\ell$ to a basis $\ell,\ell_1,\dots,\ell_n$ and define $g$ to be $g(x)=(\ell(x),\ell_1(x),\dots,\ell_n(x))$; this has the desired properties.

    Thus $g(x_i)$ all lie in $D_{\bP^m}(X_1)\cong\bA^m$.
    Then $x_i$ all lie in $g^{-1}D_{\bP^m}(X_1)$, which is still affine since $g$ is an automorphism.
    \qed

\eproof

Now we can show that $Y$ is projective.
The map $\nu\colon Y\to\cl X$ is a birational equivalence of curves.
The domain of definition of $\nu^{-1}$ has dimension $1$ (it is dense), and thus its complement has dimension $0$ since if it were $1$ then as a closed set it would equal all of $\cl X$.
Thus the complement of $\nu^{-1}$'s domain of definition is a finite subset $S\subseteq\cl X$.

So $\nu$ defines an isomorphism between $Y-\nu^{-1}S\to\cl X-S$; let $y_1,\dots,y_n=\nu^{-1}S$ (because $\nu$ is finite, it is quasi-finite).
By the previous lemma, there is an affine neighborhood $V\subseteq\cl X$ containing $S$.
Let $W=\nu^{-1}V\subseteq Y$ which is affine as well since $\nu$ is finite.
We can then embed $W$ into $\bP^m$, and because $W$ is normal as an open subset of $Y$, it extends to a map $\psi\colon Y\to\bP^m$.

Let $\phi$ be the composition $Y\xto\nu\bar X\inj\bP^r$.
Then consider the map $\Phi=(\phi,\psi)\colon Y\to\bP^r\times\bP^n$.
We claim that this is a closed embedding.

Now $Y$ is complete because $\nu\colon Y\to\cl X$ is finite and $\cl X$ is projective, and thus complete, $Y$ is complete by the previous lemma.
So $\Phi$ is a morphism from a complete to a separated variety, and thus has closed image $Z=\Phi(Y)$.
Further, $\phi$ separates any two points of $Y$ unless they are the $y_i$, in which case $\psi$ does.
So $\Phi$ is an injection.

Projection onto the first component yields $\phi$, which is an isomorphism excluding the $y_i$.
And projection onto the second component yields $\psi$, which is an isomorphism on $W$.
Thus there is an inverse $Z\ratto Y$ whose domain of definition contains all of $Z$.
Thus $\Phi$ has an isomorphism, meaning $Y\cong Z$ which is projective.

This concludes the proof of essential surjectivity and the following theorem.

\bthrm

    There is an antiequivalence of categories
    $$ \set{\hbox{irreducible smooth projective curves}} \xtos\sim \set{\hbox{finite field extensions $L/g$ of transcendence degree $1$}} $$
    given by $X\mapsto k(X)$ and $\phi\mapsto\phi^*$.

\ethrm
